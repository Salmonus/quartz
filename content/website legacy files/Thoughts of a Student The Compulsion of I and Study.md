# Thoughts of a Student: The Compulsion of "I" and "Study"

### The Daily Cycle of Thought

* Thoughts while showering in the morning.
* Thoughts while sitting on the toilet.
* Thoughts while eating alone during lunch, or after the meal.
* Thoughts during dinner, or while walking back to the classroom afterward.
* Thoughts before falling asleep on a weekday night.

In all these moments, the subject is always **"I"** and the object is **"Study."** *(I... study...)*

> **Examples:** > "I studied well today."
> "In order for me to study well, I need to take a mock exam during Night Study 1 and focus on neutralization reactions during Night Study 2."
> "The reason I failed to study well today is..."

---

### The Social Friction

When I am with friends, there are times when I cannot maintain these thoughts starting with "I... study..." Those moments pass by mindlessly with laughter or through discussions for a specific purpose. While those times are enjoyable, they leave a lingering regret once they end. And the thought that follows that regret is, predictably, "I... study..." These are often negative.

The problem is that while this **compulsive self-suggestion** practically helps my actual studying only about once or twice out of thirty times, it paralyzes every other aspect of my life. I forget how to greet others properly or how to be grateful for things that deserve gratitude.

Because of this, I often find myself spacing out while walking through the hallways, or displaying a poor attitude. This very attitude once brought me to the brink of a formal disciplinary meeting with a teacher and caused many conflicts with friends. This is because, while preoccupied with the thought "I... study...", I cannot think deeply about the words I speak. My voice drops into a blunt, low tone, and my speech and non-verbal expressions become defensive or aggressive.

On the surface, I might appear mature and cold-headed, but anyone who speaks with me for a moment can feel that my mind is elsewhere. Consequently, I only feel comfortable around people who are considerate of my mental state—those who can suppress my sunken energy with their innate positivity while meeting me at my eye level. I do not hesitate to reach out to such people. Since there are fewer than two or three such people in the entire school, my social life is a constant cycle of either quick abandonment or obsession toward them. Naturally, the self-loathing is significant.

---

### The Family and the Drive

My family also harbors a strong resentment toward these traits of mine. I have observed this since middle school and felt a deep sense of pity, but every time that feeling arose, it transformed into a "driving force" that led back to "I... study..."

If I were to summarize that motivation into a sentence, it would be:

**"The reason I must study is that I have failed to show a 'human' side to my family and those around me, so I must compensate for it through sheer competence."**

Fortunately, there are peers at school who see through my struggles—those who sincerely hope for my recovery into a normal life and offer genuine encouragement and candid advice. Spending the week at HAFS (Hankuk Academy of Foreign Studies) until Friday allows me to at least develop a sense of problem awareness regarding this "I... study..." obsession. That is why I am organizing these thoughts on a Friday evening.

However, the moment I see my family members avoiding me or frowning at me, it reverts to: **"As I thought, I have no choice but to study."** That thought turns into a sort of resolution over Saturday and Sunday. When Monday comes, I find myself in the Year 2, Class 5 classroom, more focused than ever, with an expression more grim than ever.

---

### A Shelter Called "Study"

"I... study..." is a psychological defense mechanism and a sanctuary against everything that hinders—or has the potential to hinder—my learning. The irony is that I don't actually value studying that much. The more meaningful life I dream of is to live a very ordinary, happy life without destroying my character through these undesirable thoughts and behaviors. Honestly, opening a book could come after that. I respect and deeply envy those who can live that way. I gave up on living like that a long time ago.

As my methods of self-suggestion become more sophisticated, I feel things going further astray. I know that even if I speak of these thoughts to those I can talk to, all I will receive is bitter criticism. They will say it is because I, as a student preparing for exams, lack willpower. Then, I will simply grit my teeth and think again: *"I must study."*

---

### Conclusion

In conclusion, I will be satisfied simply by doing what a student can do in my current position (studying), and if this is what happiness is, I will accept it. I will live each day as if it were my last. Doesn't the world need people like me? John Nash suffered from schizophrenia, and Turing committed suicide. Compared to them, I am a version so weak it's embarrassing to even make the comparison.

I am carrying perhaps a single speck of dust from the massive psychological burden that those legendary geniuses carried until death. Having already surpassed the limits of my intelligence and achieved many things through sheer personality alone—if this is my fate, I only hope that the world needs me. If not, why was I not given a "human" life? Or is this place hell?

I should get to sleep soon so I can study tomorrow.
