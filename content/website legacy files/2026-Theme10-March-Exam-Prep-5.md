### II. Law of Quantity into Quality and the Virtuous Cycle of Immersion

I have a green notebook that I always carry with me. I’ve done this since last year. Sometimes it wasn't green; sometimes it was an audio format with bone conduction earphones, and once I even wore latex gloves and wrote Latin words on them with a marker to memorize. The fact that I always carried an L-shaped folder even to the bathroom was famous at the study hall I attended in middle school.

On the other hand, I can barely make eye contact when dealing with people. It’s not that I do it intentionally out of disrespect, but because I simply don't use the part of the brain that should be allocated to interpersonal skills. I am very shy and uncomfortable around others, even as I approach adulthood. As long as I maintain my current lifestyle, I will live like this forever.

**The Law of Quantity into Quality (量質轉化).** This means that a gradual change in quantity accumulates until it reaches a certain stage, causing a leap in quality. This qualitative leap, in turn, induces a new gradual change in quantity. This is the name of the law I have been instinctively realizing and acting upon.

This concept was spoken of by the German philosopher **Hegel**. When quantity overflows, a leap occurs; when a certain amount accumulates, a qualitative leap is achieved at some point.

**The difference in interest is the difference in talent.** Essentially, what you devote your interest to will eventually develop into overwhelming talent over time. Those who fail to do so are called "ordinary people." Since ordinary people lack innate interest in a specific field, they must exercise their will to redirect their focus. In my case, this meant causing a qualitative change from a "Humanities brain" with no mathematical talent to a state of focusing on STEM-oriented problem-solving.

Contrary to generally "desirable" study methods, I highly prefer looking at the answer keys in Math. I collect not only my wrong answers but also problems I got stuck on, problems I got right but interpreted incorrectly, and even problems that just made me feel bad while solving. I write the problem itself word-for-word and transfer most of the solution process—including calculations—into a single notebook. More than 70% of my study focuses on looking at the answer key, interpreting it, and memorizing it, rather than struggling with the workbook. I even write down the solution first, even if I don't fully understand it during that session. It’s a method most "STEM-minded" students would never use.

**My real study begins when the formal study hours end.** I resolve the parts concerning understanding and internalizing the solutions outside of my desk. During travel time, waiting time, mealtime, and even in the bathroom—almost all available time—I repeat the contents of my consolidated notebook more than five times. Simply re-reading the highlighted parts has the effect of re-solving the problem. Even with limited intelligence, I can walk a path I have already traveled once. I repeat this 5, 10, or 100 times.

> "Water undergoes a qualitative change from liquid to vapor when it reaches the critical point of 100°C.
> What’s important here is the effort of boiling it without stopping until it reaches 100°C."

When my brain qualitatively changes in the direction I want through the accumulation of quantity, my immersion naturally increases. Through increased immersion, I can increase the quantity even more. As quantity increases, the speed of qualitative change also accelerates. At first, I feel foolishly slow. When I realize that my 200% effort is less than 2% of someone else’s effort—or even less than their breathing—I feel a sense of self-loathing and want to die. But before I know it, 200% becomes 150%, 100%, 30%... and I have transformed into the person I longed to be. Simply by making the effort to increase quantity, my nerves have been rearranged, and the person named Kim Yeon-woo has transformed into someone entirely different from before.

**"More Is Different."**
— *Philip Warren Anderson, 1972 Nobel Laureate in Physics*

I believe I have almost arrived. The level of the overwhelmingly brilliant individuals I could only look up to before coming to HAFS is starting to come within reach. Perhaps I am being arrogant because it’s been a long time since I focused on GPA. However, it is also true that the level required for GPA and the level required for the CSAT are different. I’m not confidently asserting this without evidence. I just feel it—that the qualitative change is almost complete.

I would be lying if I said I never had regrets. Why didn't I study early for the advanced learning, competitive math, or all the natural science subjects related to entrance exams? Why did I waste time copying answer keys and playing games or reading books instead of studying when my mother told me to?

These strikingly cliché regrets are not only self-destructive but also prevent me from seeing other facets of life that deserve attention. To be even more honest, what I am doing now—claiming to study properly as a senior—is still essentially "copying the answer key." But through games, I was able to grasp my own tendencies and predict how I would live my life. What gave me practical strength during my hardest moments wasn't close friends, but the game OSTs or novel characters I heard and read as a child. Ultimately, the source of my power to capture people's attention based on common values is the sensitivity I cultivated before middle school.

It seems that none of us at HAFS worrying about the CSAT right now are the "geniuses of the century" destined to lead breakthroughs in natural science or English literature. I’m not saying this out of contempt, but because interest determines talent. If a talent in a specific field reaches a level where it is powerfully manifested in connection with ego formation, the trajectory of that person's life will undoubtedly be entirely different from that of an ordinary person. And **the CSAT clearly belongs to ordinary people.** Let us all preparing for the CSAT admit that we are ordinary people.

The remaining 240 days of my senior year will be decorated by the Law of Quantity into Quality and the virtuous cycle of immersion. I will experience as many problems as possible, write them down by hand, and internalize them by immersing myself in every moment of daily life. Immersion is possible because, during the non-immersed periods of my life, I thoroughly examined the fact that this time would come. I am grateful that I had the luxury to do so.

### III. The Fundamental Loneliness of Being Human

It may sound cringeworthy, but humans are fundamentally lonely. It's not just those who perceive that most of society is heading in the wrong direction and want to propose a new path who are lonely. It's not just geniuses, heroes, or kings who are solitary.

I had a counseling session with my new homeroom teacher. When asked if I had a good break, I replied that it was "about average." When asked why I didn't think it was "excellent," I told them I hadn't been sleeping well at night.

Since the teacher is very kind-hearted, they asked more out of sincere concern, and I brushed it off by saying I wasn't on good terms with my parents. Essentially, it was a grade consultation before the March mock, so I couldn't drag it out any further.

But that’s not it. I’m too old to cry about fighting with my mother.

The period during which one can actively play a healthy role in life is sadly short. It’s so frighteningly short that it makes my skin crawl just thinking about it. I’m not afraid of getting old; I’m afraid of the fact that **my current state is my peak.** The scenario of a person harboring grand thoughts while possessing only the capabilities of an ordinary person as they age is a predicted doom. There is nothing I can do about it.

Even if I assume I can preserve my body for 60 years, one-third has already passed. Even one year spent with someone who doesn't share my vision feels like a waste. It is clear that the time I can remain in this world is not much. It’s not that I spend my time thinking about death, but the fact that there isn't a single person I can truly be close to—the fact that, in the end, we are all enemies—drives me to the battlefield of the mind. And on such days, I cannot sleep.
