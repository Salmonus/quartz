# Senior Story (5): Subjectivity, Expertise, and Patience

**By:** Kim Yeon-woo

**Platform:** Brunch

**TL;DR:** Like piloting an aircraft, the CSAT requires professional expertise. Students should aim for "Mode D" as described in *The Structure of World History*. The qualification of a judge is patience.

### I. Swiss Cheese (Expertise)

[Image Placeholder: Emmental Cheese / Swiss Cheese]

Imagine you are piloting an aircraft. Not a light plane, but a massive commercial jet. Every power source and hydraulic system is designed with double or triple redundancy. You see wings and tailfins engineered to withstand **1.5 times** the maximum load. You have been trained to handle every type of threat—pilot error, terrorism, natural disasters—according to the manual.

The aviation industry calls this the **"Swiss Cheese Model."** No single function bears the entire responsibility; instead, ordinary functions are repeated with massive redundancy so that the plane "keeps flying no matter what" even in an accident. Why Swiss cheese? Just as man-made machines inevitably have flaws, Swiss cheese has many holes—but it only becomes a real "gap" through the cheese when all those holes align perfectly in a row. The reason the fatal accident rate for aircraft is less than one per **100,000 hours** is that a crash is impossible unless all major safety layers fail simultaneously.

Becoming a captain, especially for a commercial airline, is said to be more than just possessing the driving skills of an F1 driver combined with the service mindset of a taxi driver. It is because you must control the **"situation of flight"** itself. The entire process from takeoff to landing is in your head like a photograph, and even under clear skies, you must constantly update that image with information regarding region, altitude, and pressure. You must feel the airframe, rely on the cabin crew without becoming dependent, recognize that every flight is an independent event, and keep extreme scenarios in mind.

Ultimately, expertise is directly linked to an individual’s philosophy of survival. Whatever the skill, it eventually shifts from being "the other" to being "the self." Pulling the control yoke might be a conscious movement at first, but it soon becomes as instinctive as raising your own arm. A specialist doctor or an experienced judge reaches a similar level where they don't just rely on isolated data or precedents but read the **"context."** While pilots, doctors, and judges are human and make mistakes, the "Aircraft," "Hospital," and "Court" are systems designed with thick margins for error.

All philosophy eventually converges into meta-philosophy—epistemology. The late author Kentaro Miura (*Berserk*), literary critic Kojin Karatani, and psychologist Daniel Kahneman (Bayesian theory) all reached the top of their fields to discuss epistemology in its most sophisticated form. Whether through drawing manga, being moved by Soseki Natsume, or questioning 20th-century binary classification—their expertise reflected their own philosophy of survival. Perhaps the highest philosophy of survival a human can possess is **meta-cognition**: the act of thought thinking about thought itself.

> **"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."**
> — *Charles Goodhart*

How should we view the CSAT? I’m not sure. The "CSAT situation"—with all the potential for student error and institutional blunders—is an event that can uproot the lives of countless people, much like a flight, a medical procedure, or a trial. What if we let the CSAT problems be solved smoothly, like a skilled pilot pulling the yoke, and leave the rest to the "airframe" and the "sky"? After all, a plane exists to fly, not just to be operated according to a manual. Exam papers don't exist just to be solved; ultimately, everything stops on November 14th—even planes don't fly—to transport students to a higher place (likely university). We must practice hard because we cannot afford to fail, but we must never forget: we are sitting in that seat not just to take a test, but **to fly.**

### II. Mode D (Subjectivity)

In his work *The Structure of World History* (2010), Kojin Karatani defines four modes of exchange:

* **Mode A:** Reciprocal base; maintaining bonds through mutual debt and repayment.
* **Mode B:** Redistribution; plunder and distribution under a vertical power structure.
* **Mode C:** Market exchange; equivalent exchange between commodities; free but alienated.
* **Mode D:** Transcendental exchange; an exchange format based on universal ethics, aiming for a Kantian "World Republic."

The South Korean examination system is a mixture of **Mode B and C.** Under the vertical power structure of the KICE (Evaluation Institute), grades are plundered and resources are redistributed. Through the market exchange value of Mode C (university ranking), knowledge is commodified and students are alienated. Students use evaluation criteria as their only metrics, and even though these are no longer "good measures," the state permits **overfitting** under the guise of "fairness."

However, this is also the "lesser evil" that reductionist education can offer. Therefore, while students must acknowledge the primary motivational value of Modes B and C, they must **strive for Mode D.** This is not a luxury; it is the path toward the salvation of Korean society.

### III. The Qualification of a Judge: Patience

[Image Placeholder: Sans from *Undertale*]

In the RPG *Undertale*, players can choose three paths: Normal, Pacifist, or Genocide. The final boss of the Genocide route is **Sans**. Because the player has killed every monster, their HP and attack power are overwhelmingly high. Furthermore, they can use the Save/Load function, making them theoretically unstoppable.

Yet, Sans—the "weakest monster" with **1 ATK and 1 DEF**—is arguably the hardest boss in the game. For every monster the player has killed, an invisible stat called **Karma** rises, allowing Sans to deal rapid "damage over time." If the player fails to dodge for even a second, half their health vanishes. Sans doesn't stop the player; he simply returns the sins they have committed. The purpose of the fight is not **Victory**, but **Judgment**.

His iconic line, *"You're gonna have a bad time,"* is not a warning; it is a **prediction**. Sans knows he cannot stop the player indefinitely. Instead, he offers a "bad time" to induce a Reset.

The **Paradigm** is the player. Paradigms repeat through history, whereas a single human only gets one chance. Even within a flawed paradigm, one must first act as an NPC, playing along and memorizing the set lines. Breaking the fourth wall is important, but if you don't recite your lines, you lose the right to participate in the play altogether.

When a player crosses the line, or when a paradigm fails to exceed the **SOTA (State-of-the-Art)**, that is when a "System Observer" like Sans must intervene. Not out of a sense of justice to save the world, but out of a bitter sense of responsibility: *"I have to be the one to stop you."* As the mission of the only one who remembers everything. At that point, who knows? The player might withdraw their wrong choice. Until then, one must endure and function as an NPC. That is the qualification of a judge who stands against a god.
