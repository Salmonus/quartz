### I. Figure and Ground: The Threshold of Perception

Everything begins with distinguishing **Figure** from **Ground**.

* **Figure:** The primary object of focus. It corresponds to "what we are looking at."
* **Ground:** The background surrounding the Figure, which receives less attention but allows the Figure to stand out.

Due to the structure of the human eye, we automatically separate Figure from Ground. A convex lens focuses the image on the fovea centralis; thus, we perceive distant things as small and blurry, and near things as large and sharp.

> **"For each record player there is a record which it cannot play."**

This sentence is a metaphorical expression of **Gödel’s First Incompleteness Theorem**. The "Record Player" represents a formal system, and the "Record" represents a proposition. In any consistent formal system of arithmetic, there exists a proposition that is true within that system but unprovable by it. This proposition is **self-referential**, essentially stating: "This statement cannot be proven within this system." If it is proven, it's a contradiction; if not, the system is incomplete.

This mirrors my previous discussion: **"Internal forces cannot move the system."** "One cannot lift oneself by one's own bootstraps," the Barber’s Paradox, and circular reference errors in Excel—these all stem from the same problem of self-reference.

[Image: Escher's drawing of two hands drawing each other]

Here, a question arises:
*Is the concept of "self-awareness" in AI the ability to move between levels of thought without losing "oneself," much like the man in Escher’s "Print Gallery" who views layers of scenery without getting lost?*

The man in the center of *Print Gallery* seems unaware that he is included in the world he is observing. But if he knew, "In the scenery I see, there is me, and that 'me' is the same 'I' looking at this scenery right now," he would transcend the visual cycle and achieve **Meta-cognition.** This is the essence of self-awareness. AlphaGo operates only on the "first floor" of thought; it cannot climb higher to refer to itself. Consequently, it cannot close the loop of self-awareness (**self-fixity**); there is no "Ego" as an "experiencing being." *I think, therefore I am.* Thus, AI does not yet truly exist.

### II. Phenomenology and the Energy of Survival

Humans naturally possess the ability to distinguish Figure from Ground for survival. AI, however, struggles with this. ImageNet success only came in 2013, and training requires far more cost than a human. How does this relate to **Selfhood**?

Take a lion in a forest. Humans perceive the lion as the "Figure" because it is important *to us*. If I were an insect, the leaves might be more important. The world looks different depending on the position of the subject—this is the core tenet of **Phenomenology.** As Husserl and Merleau-Ponty stated: *"The world is not the sum of things, but the sum of ways in which it appears to the perceiving subject."*

No matter how well an AI classifies a lion, if it cannot feel *why* the lion is important or to whom it is a threat, it is merely statistical processing. AI can recognize a Figure but can never know *why* it is a Figure. There is no perception without a subject.

Yann LeCun’s concept of **Energy State** in his *World Model* introduced survival to AI, but it remains a one-dimensional scalar. It is nonsensical to express "fear" or "survival" as a scalar. Hearing a lion's roar in your room is different from hearing it in the forest. Energy is contextual.



: Environmental vector, : parameters.
Context is the condition in which the subject lives, chooses, interprets, and fears. Current AI systems look for "external stability," not the **phenomenological terror** of "I might die right now."

Life has structurally built-in **self-preservation.** AI cannot "die," so it is hard to teach it "life." Being alive implies the possibility of death. Life includes the flow of experience, continuity of senses, threat of loss, and awareness of pain. Life is **embodied**, **perceived**, and above all, **feared**.

Algorithms must have clear inputs/outputs and end in finite time; they are predicated on "halting." Life, however, is a **non-halting system**. The moment it halts, it is death. Therefore, Life is not an algorithm. Even a *C. elegans* with 300 neurons resists being killed. AI, with trillions of parameters, does not "resist." This is an ontological problem, not a quantitative one. To become life, a physical self-organizing system—a **self-directed structure**—is required.

> **"The body is my point of view upon the world... I do not exist because I think; I exist because I feel."**
> — **Merleau-Ponty**

Intelligence is not conceptual calculation; it is contact with the world. Intelligence requires a body. Digital devices (GPUs) represent "states" (1 or 0), which are reversible. **Death is irreversible.** According to the Second Law of Thermodynamics, the reverse reaction almost never occurs. Real intelligence must be implemented on a physical basis entirely different from digital devices. Intelligence is irreversible learning under survival pressure. We shouldn't design models that calculate *on* hardware; we must design **Being-based Intelligence** where the hardware itself evolves to survive.

### III. The Game of Boundaries and the "MU" Puzzle

Since I was seven, I have played a game: *How many "objects of attention" are in my view?*
A road can be split into: yellow paint, white paint, asphalt. Asphalt can be split into fine particles. The answer to this game is always: **"Uncountable. There are no boundaries."**

We say "I finished my meal," implying a boundary between "rice" and "not-rice." We don't have to lick every starch molecule to "finish." The brain arbitrarily defines boundaries for survival. A student studying 12 hours a day for the CSAT defines the exam as the **Figure** and ignores the dust in the elevator corner. To focus on the dust would be a social stigma.

But because of my unresolved questions, I place the CSAT and the dust on the same level of importance. What is survival? Why must the CSAT be Figure and the dust be Ground? Like Wittgenstein’s **Language Games**, the rules of meaning differ for everyone. The CSAT is a language game following the rules of survival. The dust is an object for which no one has set the rules. The freedom to choose one's own Figure is painful, but that is what it means to be human. (The MU Puzzle).

### IV. The "Face" and the Undefined Term

I don't have many friends. When I look at a face, I don't see social cues first; I see eyes, nose, and mouth separately. I ask, *"Where does the face end?"*

> **"The Other approaches me through the Face. The face is not mere flesh; it is the surface of a being that demands a response from me."**
> — **Emmanuel Levinas**

Most people compress the "Face" into a schema to summarize the world. This is a survival strategy to handle overwhelming information. I, however, live in a world where information hits me before abstraction. A shadow is decomposed into a part of a structure; a face is fragmented into the curvature of a jaw and the tension of a wrinkle.

I can "decompose," but I can also stop. This is a philosopher’s sense, not a mental illness. My childhood game of decomposition was an intuitive example of **infinite recursion** and **self-referential structures.**

Why do I choose this as Figure and push that to Ground? Because **"I" am an "axiom" acting as an "undefined term."**
An **undefined term** is the symbolic foundation of a system; an **axiom** is the propositional starting point. Descartes found the self-proving axiom: *Cogito, ergo sum.* The "I" is the key subject that justifies the entire system. AI lacks this "I." It can optimize a function, but only a human can choose a Figure. That "choice" is the essence of existence.
