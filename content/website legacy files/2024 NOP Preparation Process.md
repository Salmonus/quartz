### 1. Stage Logistics and Management

In the process of preparing multiple teams, several logistical details required close attention:

* **Stage Transitions and Placement:** I had to monitor entry/exit paths, ensuring symmetry relative to the stage. I also checked for potential safety hazards, the appropriateness of bowing/greetings, visibility from the audience's perspective, and the overall facial expressions and atmosphere of the performers.
* **Rehearsal Coordination:** While one team is rehearsing, the next team must be on standby. To manage this effectively, one must have the entire schedule and order memorized. It’s not just about waiting; the stage staff needs to know the exact number of microphones and chairs required in advance and be ready to assist in moving equipment on and off stage. Teams needed to react reflexively to specific MC cues (e.g., "Please come out now!") to minimize wasted time between performances.
* **Audio Management:** The audio technician must remain attentive to the external environment, adjusting levels so they aren't too loud or too quiet. Although I wasn't in charge of audio, I observed that the technician stayed in the video control room, communicated constantly with the stage team, and responded immediately when called.
* *Cue Recording:* It was vital to record the specific starting cues for each team (e.g., lifting their head, saying "let's go," tapping a foot, giving an 'OK' sign, or starting with a video projection).


* **Equipment and Instruments:** While the variety of props isn't vast, the absence or failure of a single item is fatal. Standard gear includes keyboards, amps, standing mics, chairs, guitars, hand mics, drums, and music stands.
* *Placement:* Large items like keyboards and drums are difficult to move quickly, so their positions were often pre-negotiated with the performance teams. Other items were moved on the fly.
* *Categorization by Team Type:*
* **Dance Teams:** No equipment needed.
* **Vocal-only Teams:** Only mics required (according to headcount).
* **Band Teams:** High demand for gear (keyboards, amps, chairs, music stands, standing/hand mics). Layout must be carefully considered.
* **Others (Traditional music, soloists, etc.):** Usually bring their own unique equipment; minimal work for the committee.

### 2. Administrative and Physical Labor

* **Data and Ticketing:** We used Google Forms for responses, but the order of data got scrambled, requiring manual sorting. We also need to research how to print sequential tickets in bulk. We considered the pros and cons of pre-ticketing versus on-site ticketing. We allowed one person to register up to four companions, and seating was chosen based on the order of application.
* **Role Division:** Labor was divided into line management, stage staff, mic management, audio, entry/exit management, 2nd-floor management, and indoor staff.
* **Site Prep:** We measured the gymnasium dimensions in advance to calculate and arrange the exact number of chairs needed.

### 3. Personal Reflection: A Mismatch of Competency

Everything requires genuine interest to be done well. I found it difficult to work because I was surrounded by music I don't usually listen to. However, I realized that other members weren't necessarily participating just because they were full of energy or excited about the festival. Ultimately, one's contribution is determined by **situational judgment, coping skills, problem-solving abilities, and a sense of responsibility.**

**I failed to contribute.** Because this is a school, we don't rank contributions to determine pay. But if this were a company, I would undoubtedly be the first person to be fired. I lack the necessary competency. Even organizing what I learned today into this list won't immediately improve my abilities. Through today's experience, I realized I severely lack the ability to coordinate affairs between people. If I am offered a similar role in the future, I will decline.

The reason I joined the **Event Planning Committee** was that I knew I would face many occasions to plan and organize events in the future. Even if it's not a festival, people gather to promote or commemorate things. I believed a leader should have the competency to judge what to do and communicate effectively with both superiors and subordinates.

However, in those moments, my mind goes blank, and I feel dizzy. Honestly, even while preparing for the festival, I couldn't stop thinking about the CSAT and my studies. I lack the ability to read social relationships or judge holistic situations, but I possess immense obsession and focus on a single task. **I realized I am meant to be an engineer.** If I choose to decline event planning roles in the future, I must further refine my technical craft to do my part, and always think about how I can contribute even if I cannot lead.

I don't feel good. I tried to look at this positively, but I can't shake the feeling that this isn't for me. While there's no absolute rule that one must contribute through "work," I observed that those who "free-ride" in terms of labor were at least socially adept—they got along with everyone, offered comfort or encouragement, and created synergy just by being there. I am not that type either. If I don't contribute through work, I simply become an awkward presence.

I should give up on these types of roles and adjust my career trajectory accordingly.
