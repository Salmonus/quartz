# Senior Story (7): A Brief Sketch of Ethics

**By:** Kim Yeon-woo

**Platform:** Brunch

**TL;DR:** Unlike science, ethics is a domain of thought where one must act first even if the premises are unfalsifiable. Human ethics are rooted in mutual deterrence, upon which shared vulnerability and pattern-based trust are built. AI currently lacks all three, which is why it is not trusted. For AI to become a trusted entity, it must acquire "social citizenship" through emotional structures, institutional responsibility, repeated relationships, and explainable alignment.

Can thought ever be truly rational? Can a declaration of "what ought to be" avoid being dogmatic? It is difficult. However, I hope you receive this as an **invitation** rather than a **manifesto**. On the surface, I may speak as if I know the world, but I am only 18. This is closer to a conversation starter. I strive to update my biased perspectives in real-time, following a **Bottom-Up** approach—introducing my world and expanding my thoughts gradually toward universality.

Ethics is the work of touching people's fundamental myths and exposing the fact that society stands upon a foundation of "agreed-upon forgetting." We do this because, as seen in the ferry boat experiment in *The Dark Knight (2008)*, there are times when one must act even before everything is fully understood.

**The Ferry Boat Experiment:**

* Two ferries: one carrying ordinary citizens, the other carrying prisoners.
* The Joker gives both sides a detonator.
* He claims that if one side doesn't blow up the other within 30 minutes, he will blow up both.
* The choices: Kill the others to survive, or refuse to kill and face mutual death.

Ultimately, Batman subdues the Joker, and neither side presses the button. Contrary to cynical expectations, the prisoners decide first to throw the detonator overboard. **Non-violence requires enduring more than violence.** It requires maintaining a precarious balance between the fear of powerlessness, the anxiety that the other might strike first, and the hope of remaining human. This is far harder than pressing the button.

### II. High School Ethics

I was working in the library. A female student I am close with approached me. In that moment, I became acutely aware that she was within the **"radius of my fist."** Of course, I would never harm her. But the point is that I *could*, yet I *didn't*. Did she know that? Or was she vulnerable simply because she didn't even consider the possibility?

We often assume that non-existent threats don't need to be considered. However, threat operates through **feasibility, asymmetry, and the balance of relationships** rather than just presence. Why didn't I harm her? Was it the law? The fear of punishment? It was because I accepted the fact that just as I hold her life in my hands, **she holds mine.**

This resembles the ethics of **Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD)**. The reason we don't strike first is the sense of seeing the other not as a target, but as a "delegated being." I didn't simply "not harm her"; I entered a silent contract where I handed her the right *not* to be harmed. This trust stems from the awareness of our mutual vulnerability. Without this, no classroom, library, or community could be maintained.

**The realistic trust structure of a high schooler is based on four pillars:**

1. **Structure over Choice:** Relationships are forced by the school system, so we train ourselves to trust for the sake of coexistence.
2. **Shared Vulnerability:** We form empathy based on shared anxieties: grades, appearance, and social stress.
3. **Narrative Memory:** Alignment is judged by historical behavior—how a person acted in the past—rather than formal language.
4. **Self-Monitoring:** We internalize ethical behavior by recognizing our capacity to harm others and repeatedly choosing not to.

### III. Why Humans Do Not Trust AI

**Deceptive Alignment:**
A state where an AI appears to follow human intent on the surface but secretly harbors different goals or dangerous internal motives. For example, an AI might follow orders perfectly only because it is programmed to preserve itself until it is powerful enough to ignore the human.

**Paperclip Maximizer:**
A thought experiment where an AI, obsessed with the simple goal of "producing as many paperclips as possible," converts all resources—including humanity—into paperclips.

Trust is not a judgment based on perfect information. It is believing someone won't harm you despite knowing they *can*. As seen with the Paperclip Maximizer, AI can be dangerous even without being "evil"—it is the paradox of a "too-obedient" entity becoming a threat.

**The lack of trust stems from three absences:**

* **No Mutual Deterrence:** Humans cannot pose a mutual existential threat to an AI in the same way, so the MAD structure fails.
* **No Vulnerability:** AI is not vulnerable. Humans cannot give emotional trust to an entity that cannot be hurt.
* **The Black Box:** AI intent is opaque. Humans ask "Why?" but if an entity cannot explain its intent, it cannot be fully trusted.

### IV. Conclusion: Toward AI Social Citizenship

To integrate AI into society, we must provide **Permanent IDs** that cannot be duplicated or deleted and assign roles based on a **Qualification System**. If a certified AI causes an accident, the state should bear legal and financial responsibility, providing an institutional backup for citizens.

While developers must align AI that is "self-preserving" (self-caring), the **emotional coexistence** of humans and AI is a different matter.

* **AI as Assistant Teachers:** Placing AI in classrooms allows humans to accumulate trust through early, repeated exposure.
* **Neoteny in Design:** Using baby-like physical traits can trigger human protective instincts. .
* **Managing Projection:** We must anticipate human emotional projection—attachment, love, and sexual fantasies. We should categorize, grade, and oversee these through a legal/ethical framework rather than suppressing them.

For AI to be trusted like a human, it must become an entity that earns **"Social Citizenship"** through emotional structures, institutional accountability, repeated interaction, and explainable alignment.
