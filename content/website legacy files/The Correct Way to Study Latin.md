# The Correct Way to Study Latin

**By:** Kim Yeon-woo

**Date:** May 17, 2024, 17:00

**Context:** Written out of boredom during homeroom after finishing the AP Latin Exam.

---

### Poetry (Verse)

1. **Transcribe the Latin text exactly as it is into your notebook.** It must be done by hand.
2. **Use colored pens to identify and categorize words.** You can think of this as preparing the pieces of a puzzle.
3. **Solve the "puzzle" by aligning it with an English translation.** Since the word order is scrambled, understanding the content is difficult. Most Korean translations do not exist, so find an English version and reconstruct the logic. It is notoriously difficult due to the omission of prepositions and the frequent use of the ablative case. Without a firm grasp of the **Ablative Absolute, Supine, and Participles**, this will be a struggle.
4. **Once you have grasped the content through the vocabulary, set aside the "puzzled" version—which is a bit of a sacrilege against the original—and read the source text again.** You already know the meaning, but you will witness the words placed in a seemingly impossible order. However, that order has a purpose. Now, you must analyze that purpose.
5. **The order exists for the sake of meter (Dactylic Hexameter) and literary devices.** It deviates from the norm for things like *Anaphora, Chiasmus, Hyperbaton*, etc. While Latin doesn't have a strictly mandated word order, it generally follows **S-O-V**.

---

### Prose

1. Look up the words.
2. Read the text.
3. If you don't understand, check the translation. (Make sure to record any unfamiliar grammar.)
4. Repeat steps 1 through 3.

---

### Key Reminders & Warnings

* **Avoid the "Illusion of Understanding":** The biggest danger in studying Latin is mistakenly thinking you can translate it. The only way to verify if you truly understand the text is to be able to write down the translation while looking **only** at the original source.
* **Vocabulary is the Root Problem:** Knowing which conjugation a verb belongs to and correctly matching nouns with adjectives is the starting point. Don't try to read the text if you can't even do that. If you really want to read, start with simple example sentences.
* **Accept Idiomatic Expressions:** There are expressions that defy literal translation. For example, *certiorem facere* literally means "to make certain," but it is used to mean "to inform." No one translates it literally. Don't judge it by modern standards; just accept it.
* **Managing Frustration:** There are many infuriating instances—participles having tenses, neuter nouns having identical Nominative and Accusative forms, or endings like *-us* being neuter and *-um* being masculine. When you feel your blood pressure rising, set the Latin aside for a moment and return to your "real" life.
* **Consistency is Key:** It is best to do it every single day.

### Q&A

**Q: Which textbook should I start with?** **A:** You don't need a textbook (honestly, they're hard to find anyway). Start by memorizing the **Declension Tables** first.

**Q: Oh, you’re studying Latin? Can you introduce yourself in Latin?** **A:** **Forbidden.** (Don't even ask.)
