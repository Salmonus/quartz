# AP Latin Exam Review

**By:** Kim Yeon-woo

**Date:** May 17, 2024, 16:45

**Timeline:** Prepared for the AP Latin exam for 10 days, from last Wednesday (May 8) to today (May 17).

---

### Overview

The exam covers a volume equivalent to a full college semester. The syllabus includes selected portions of:

* **Vergil’s *Aeneid***: Books 1, 2, 4, and 6.
* **Caesar’s *Gallic War***: Books 1, 4, 5, and 6.
If you include the English reading requirements and additional granular grammar points, the actual scope feels even broader.

**Conclusion: It is difficult.**

According to *Namuwiki* (a Korean wiki), the workload is immense. While the description of the scope is accurate, the FRQs (Free Response Questions) are not "practically impossible" as some suggest. Since the questions are based on a fixed syllabus and consist of translation and short answers, you can answer quite accurately if you have pre-memorized the material.

---

### The Exam Experience

#### Multiple Choice Questions (MCQ)

When I entered the testing room, I was the only one there. The MCQ section has 50 questions:

* About 10 questions each from the *Aeneid* and the *Gallic War*.
* **Sights (Unseen passages):** One poem and one prose passage. These were surprisingly "polite"—the word order was straightforward, and a vocabulary list was provided.

**Difficulties:**

* **Scansion (Meter) and Ablative uses:** These were challenging. Although I studied the *Aeneid* in great detail, I don't think I performed as well as I hoped on the MCQs.
* **Caesar vs. Vergil:** Conversely, I only skimmed the English summary of Caesar's *Gallic War* right before the exam, yet I found it manageable. One passage was about the Britons' chariot tactics; even without a perfect Latin translation, I could deduce the answers from having read the English text of Book 4.
* **The Vergil Puzzle:** In the *Aeneid*, the word order is so scrambled that questions often ask which word modifies which. There’s really no way around it other than total memorization. Since English and Latin word orders are almost polar opposites, matching the two is like solving a puzzle where you have to rewrite the *Aeneid* from scratch in your head.

#### Literary Devices

A flood of obscure literary devices appeared. I knew this would be a weak point since I hadn't studied them deeply. While I could identify *Alliteration, Anaphora, Chiasmus,* and *Hyperbole* on the spot, I was lost on the others. (And of course, the "others" showed up).

---

### Free Response Questions (FRQ)

We were given 15 minutes of reading time and 1 hour and 45 minutes of writing time. It was "difficult" in the sense that I ran out of things to write. I finished an hour early.

1. **Translation/Short Answer:** The first two focused on the journey to the Underworld (*Aeneid* Book 6) and the role of the Druids (Caesar). Despite having read these before the exam, I couldn't recall them with 100% precision, so I likely lost points. Ironically, the Druid passage was an MCQ past paper from 2023.
2. **Analytical Essay:** This was intense. If I hadn't analyzed Book 1 down to the individual word level, I would have had nothing to say. The prompt required a comparison between the **storm caused by Juno's wrath** in the *Aeneid* and the **storm as a natural phenomenon** in the *Gallic War*, focusing on how each affected the respective journeys.
* I filled the space by outlining the plot and structured my conclusion based on **divine intervention, literary flair, and the severity of the damage.**
* The writing was far from a perfect "essay" and clearly read like the work of a non-native speaker, but I'm curious to see how it will be scored.


3. **Short Answer (Context):** I hadn't specifically studied the use of *ut*-clauses or certain ablatives. I also missed a question about the English reading requirement (outside the Latin text). Regarding Dido's reason for moving, I gave a rather nonsensical answer. Fortunately, the Book 4 excerpt featured the word *laetissima* (which I associated with "most sad" in context, though it usually means happiest), so I guessed "Death." If it was the wedding, I'm doomed.

### Reflection

It was exhausting. I studied with an intensity I’ve never experienced before—even on my days off.

> **A moment of fury:** I remember being so confused why someone mentioned 14 nymphs when I only saw the word *septem* (seven), completely ignoring the *bis* (twice) right in front of it. Looking back at my notes now (*bis septem*), I feel a surge of rage at my past self. Thankfully, it didn't show up on the test.

Recently, someone in the International Track asked me why I’m doing this. I don’t particularly care about the score. I don’t think this will significantly impact my college admissions or "success" in life.

**But if such beautiful poetry didn't exist, what would be the point of having all the money in the world?**

I was happy while studying, I am happy now, and I am satisfied. The proctor watched me for two hours while I sat there smiling at my exam paper. I must have looked like a total creep.

I love things that are no longer "useful," like Latin, and I will continue to study them. Once my college entrance exams are over, I plan to read other epics in Ancient Greek. Sanskrit is also on my list.
