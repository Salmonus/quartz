### I. Result of March Mock Prep: Addicted to New Structures

[Image Placeholder: A stylishly designed textbook cover]

*(The cover designs are becoming true pieces of art.)*

Even John von Neumann said that in mathematics, you don't understand things, you just get used to them. Beyond superficial memorization, the completion of memorization is **inference**. For example, to select the shape of a quartic function that has an inflection point satisfying specific conditions, all four basic shapes must be ingrained in your mind. Ultimately, math is—at least initially—a memorization subject. Science electives (Gwatam) are the same.

When your study reaches a certain level, there comes a moment when you actually *want* to take a test. It’s that feeling when you believe the patterns you've memorized are valid and that your skills are superior to others.

When I was in elementary school, I fought a character like this in a game. The patterns were incredibly fast, and the damage was cumulative. You had to memorize every single movement, essentially learning a new pattern with every "Game Over" screen. If you didn't know he was coming and failed to save enough healing items, you were in for a world of pain.

* When you first meet him, you dodge a little and inevitably die. You think, *"How is this even beatable?"*
* With more effort, you reach the "Mercy Event" where the patterns change. This is the second point where your mental strength is tested because the difficulty spikes exponentially from there.
* With even more effort, you become skilled enough to have surplus food items even after the mercy event. The more items you have, the higher your survival rate.
* Once you can dodge every bone attack without taking a single hit before the mercy event, you get to experience the attacks just before the "Final Special Move." You can't give up here.
* After surviving the bizarre "Gaster Blasters" (or 'Gaster Windmills') that are impossible to escape once caught, you finally face the "Final Special Move." At that point, you've essentially won.

There’s a clear temperature difference in how my mother looks at me when I'm gaming versus when I'm opening a book. Her reaction to seeing me dodge a skeleton monster's attacks versus seeing me solve a science mock exam with a timer determines what’s for dinner.

However, I believe the two are essentially the same. The "patterns" of the boss monster I memorized as a child correspond to the "type-based learning" of Science electives. Patterns are combinations of random variables that manifest differently based on the player’s HP, increasing the difficulty. The variations of past paper questions that target a student’s weaknesses are the culprits that drain your soul and stamina before you even reach the "killer questions."

Just as I saved HP recovery items in anticipation of a final move, everyone saves time for Question 20 in Science. Interestingly, if you fill your HP to the max in anticipation of the final move, Sans purposely delays his last attack to wear you down. KICE (Evaluation Institute) plays a similar role, tormenting students with these unexpected new situations.

How can a student learn KICE's patterns, reach the score just before the killer questions, survive the "final special move," and get a perfect 50? There is only one way: **learn by experiencing "Game Over" repeatedly.**

There comes a point where you’ve died so many times that your pattern recognition is beyond perfect, yet you think, *"If I can't beat it this time, I should probably just give up."* You’ve practiced so much and invested so much time that, even considering all sunk costs, you consider turning off the computer. It is in that final moment that a human can let go of everything and immerse themselves with peak concentration. In that state of trance, you realize you've already enjoyed all the fun the game has to offer. And you realize that to kill such a monster, you have become a monster yourself.

The real question is: *"Am I at that level in Science?"* Ashamedly, the answer is no. I haven't even reached the "Mercy Event." My pattern memorization isn't complete enough to save time for the final move. I haven't practiced or invested enough time to feel despair or give up just because the March Mock might go poorly. It's not because I didn't try; it's because I started late. To practice mocks with a timer, you must first be able to solve the entire range of problems with unlimited time. Reaching that baseline is a very recent achievement for me.

Throughout **Themes 1 to 4**, I struggled to "stabilize Science." I was at a "No-base" level where I couldn't even touch weekly workbooks like *Hwajaljuice* or *Stimpack*, so I had to build concepts by solving separate past paper workbooks.

But I must also accept this: regardless of the start, skill is skill, and this skill will be the most important indicator of my entrance exam journey until the June Mock. It's fine to admit a lack of skill. For instance, it's fine to think, *"I probably won't win"* when facing Sans in the final corridor. But if you intentionally take hits and lose just because you assume you'll fail, you’ll never even know that a "Mercy Event" or a "Pre-Final Attack" exists. The study direction for April–June changes entirely depending on whether you scored 25, 35, or 45.

Finally, don't overcomplicate it. Only those who enjoy facing new structures can do well on the CSAT. Naturally, the exam will feature problems you've never seen before. A "New Type" is not something completely detached from what you've learned; it’s a problem where you might hesitate for a second, but then discover the solution by applying a bit more thought to your existing methods. Whether it's overlapping magnetic fields in a triangle, dual pulleys, or a neutralization reaction with an unfamiliar ratio—the first step is always identifying the magnetic field's result, analyzing the force and acceleration direction, or judging the acidity/alkalinity. Those "Protocols" are the inner strength I’ve built through time investment, and they symbolize a learner's capacity.

Naturally, there will be anxiety. It’s the first exam of my life directly linked to the CSAT. But if I get nervous here, I won't be able to handle the real thing. There will be life events far more difficult than the CSAT; if I'm intimidated by this, there's no hope. Therefore, even for Science, where my maturity in type-based learning isn't as high as in Math, I have my own notebook where I've organized my response methods. Re-verifying my study process and reviewing the methods that can be applied to any problem is what I must do until Tuesday evening.

The greatest gift my high school life has given me is developing an eye for **Structure**. Take an apple, for example.
The ability to recognize what an apple means to a human—that it’s a delicious fruit, how red it is, how crunchy it will be, and how beautiful it is—is the **Eye for Meaning**. This is an innate talent. However, objectively identifying that an apple consists of a stem and flesh, that it has seeds about 2cm in diameter inside, and that those are the inedible parts—that is the **Eye for Structure**.

In previous Themes, I think I pointed out the problems of the Korean education system that emphasizes *only* the eye for structure. On the other hand, in **Theme 9**, I discussed how I must also possess and refine that eye for structure. Seeing structure doesn't mean being a "Type T" (per MBTI) who only speaks literally and lacks imagination. It’s the opposite. It means knowing that the world contains not only apples but also oranges, pears, and bananas, and that the method used to analyze an apple can help you guess the interior of other fruits. This is scientific thinking. It doesn't limit imagination; it allows imagination to align with reality and become even richer.
