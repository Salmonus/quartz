### The "All-In" Strategy and Its Failure

At the beginning of the semester, as soon as I received my school timetable, I filtered out the classes I wouldn't attend. This was because I firmly believed that securing as much self-study time as possible would lead to an improvement in my skills. Starting two weeks ago, I purchased the *Zaisungh* workbooks for Physics I and Chemistry I and solved almost all the problems—including past questions for seniors—that fell within the scope of the September mock exam.

Despite the cold stares from friends and teachers, I stubbornly pushed through. The result? Dismal scores and a "Letter of Promise" (disciplinary reflection).

**What went wrong?**

I believed in the power of the "All-in" strategy. Much like in a video game, a person's Skill Points or EXP are not infinite. If you don't invest them into a specific skill, you can't deal damage to any monster. Considering that, and looking back at my actions, my Chemistry and Physics scores should have been 50/50, and Korean should have been in the 70s. Based on my actual practice, that *should* have been the case.

Yet, my scores say the exact opposite. My performance in **Non-fiction**, where I invested only about 30 minutes every morning—but did so consistently since summer break—was overwhelmingly better than in the **Science Electives**, where I dumped my entire two-week daily routine and night study sessions.

---

### Making the Unconscious Conscious

I don't believe my two weeks were meaningless. I am confident that I can solve every type of question in CSAT Chemistry I and every theme in Physics I (excluding magnetic fields and the duality of light), provided there is no time limit.

**The problem is the 30-minute limit.** Because I lack the confidence to reach question number 20 on the paper, I end up making mistakes as early as question 5 or 6.

> *"Until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate."* — Carl Jung

My practice and the actual exam were not aligned 50:50. Every thought I have during self-study is only meaningful if I can pull it out in the exam room. Ultimately, to solve problems within the time limit, I must "make the unconscious conscious."

Specifically, I must habituate the initial analyses I perform for certain types of stoichiometric relationships. I already knew these were called **"Behavioral Protocols,"** I simply hadn't implemented them. What I need is discipline rooted in humility and the right kind of contemplation. While I must think constantly, it shouldn't go against my basic human needs; rather, those thoughts should focus on turning my self-study insights into practical exam-day performance.

---

### The Power of Valid Training

I wake up at 7:00 AM every day, and in the hour I gain by going to school early, I solve *Mae3Bi* (daily non-fiction practice).

This discipline is what gave me my non-fiction score. In Korean, where the only extra thing I did was solve two past papers the day before, I might have even reached Grade 1. The *Mae3Bi* workbook is structured so that you record the time taken for each passage alongside the grading, which allowed me to subconsciously monitor my speed. Ultimately, my Korean studies became **"Valid Training."**

I had planned to grind Calculus during the time gained by not going on the school field trip and throughout the Chuseok holiday. But without "valid training," my scores would have simply repeated themselves, making the effort meaningless.

---

### Future Principles

1. **Do [Subject] at [Specific Time/Place].** It is crucial to know when to stop and move on.
2. **Verify:** Did I check every case using a fast, intuitive, and implementable method?
3. **Rest:** When not studying, allow yourself to actually rest.

There are many friends who don't seem to worry as intensely as I do, who seem to study with ease and comfort, yet possess far superior skills. I must not be greedy or arrogant. Living my life and doing what I can from my current position is enough to be happy. If I win the battle against my basic instincts to secure a superior "rate of change," and if I correct my plans and actions through proper thought, then strength and intelligence will take root. I just need to wait until then. Unlike "Why," the question of **"How"** is actually that simple.

It is impossible to be tense and straining at every single moment. I will take the long view, intentionally let go of the tension, and simply keep the promises I made to myself. That is not living sloppily. It is not abandoning duty. It is not throwing away time irresponsibly.

---

### Final Summary

* **Old mindset:** Think constantly.
* **New mindset:** Take the long view, and think *properly*.
