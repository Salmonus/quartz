### 1. The Poodle

I have been in a foul mood since last Wednesday. There is no progress in my studies. I feel like my scores won’t rise, yet there’s nothing I can do about it. When I solve full-length mock exams, I either get them all right or miss just one. I rarely fail to find the answer unless I run out of time. This applies to all subjects, including the two sciences. Up until now, I acknowledged my lack of objective knowledge and focused on filling that gap. Now, the problem lies in pouring out the knowledge I’ve filled to the point of overflowing. Unlike the past, when I was anxious but found every new concept interesting, I have entered a tedious transitional period.

I’m at a stage where I know I can solve the problems, but I might still get them wrong in the actual exam hall. Or perhaps I know 70–80% of the core logic, but miss it by a hair's breadth... This repeats every time I sit at my desk. There’s also the issue of stamina. KICE will want to recognize the depth of my studies, and the vessel that showcases that skill is physical and mental stamina. I realize now that compared to my academic potential, my self-management—specifically my ability to manage time and emotions—is severely lacking. At this rate, increasing the volume of knowledge is useless for improving exam performance. Accumulating knowledge through typological learning is fundamentally different from the "Understanding" required by the CSAT. Ultimately, I have lost my way.

It’s not that there aren't problems I can't solve. However, the task of expanding my horizon of thought has become unbelievably difficult. In April of last year, I would get more than half the questions wrong in any high-level workbook, and just reading the explanations made me feel a spark of growth. Even if I lamented my low skill, I never despaired of growth itself. Rather, having peers high above me to compare myself against filled me with a sense of fulfillment as I raised my absolute value. Now, after a year of accumulation, a perfect 100 is still far away. It’s not as distant as before; I can faintly see what a perfect CSAT score looks like. But I’ve hit a transparent wall while running at full speed.

The joy of a beginner gaining +3 or +4 points by investing an hour of Night Study (Yaja) is over. Now, studying feels like nothing more than a re-verification of existing skills. In the morning, I solve *Ganssulgae* (Korean workbook). Usually, I get 4 out of 4 correct within the time limit. On the rare occasion I miss a non-fiction question, the explanation leaves me feeling "How could anyone solve that?" rather than enlightened. I start the morning homeroom with a lingering sense of discomfort. It’s the same with *Ass*, *Stimpack*, and *FAST TRACK*. The problems I get wrong incite resentment rather than awe.

As a result, I am drifting away from my studies. Today (Friday), I sat in V204 to start studying earlier than others, but a sense of helplessness and frustration I’ve never felt before swept over me. I sat there for a long time with my eyes closed and my head down. In the end, I did no work at all and returned to the dormitory, abandoning my Night Study session. Last year, this rarely happened unless I had just been rejected after a confession. A high school senior should never waste a day unless they are severely ill. The fact that I allowed this internally is the problem. It was allowed because I am losing faith in my own growth. I thought, "It's okay to waste an hour." I need to redefine "One Hour" just as I did in **Theme 1**. I must find a breakthrough.

For all these reasons, I recently started studying **Physics II**. I’m not joking; I bought the entire series of Physics II *Stimpack* (except Vol. 3 and 4) and the *Suneung Teukgang* (CSAT Special Lecture). I even listened to the torque lectures. It’s giving me back that sense of growth in the 0–40 point range I felt with Math last year or with Science during this winter break. The problem is that in Physics II, too, it’s hard to get a Grade 1 unless you resolve the existing issues. If I only try to increase the volume of knowledge without correcting my innate nature, the problem won't be solved. I end up dejected again.

Is the way I treat people any different? Or the way I treat life? The desire to do too well sometimes leads a person toward a misguided stubbornness. If that stubbornness manifests poorly or there is no adult to correct it, even the most talented person cannot grow. I’ve concluded that the emotions I feel toward the CSAT lately stem from internal limits I’ve witnessed within myself since I was very young. Unless I shatter my existing world, I cannot move forward. Unless I change my methods from the start—unless I shake the most fundamental and essential starting point—no amount of time investment will matter. Just as pouring all the text in the universe into the current Transformer architecture won't let it mimic even a 3-year-old's emotional expression, a vast amount of information is meaningless without changing the framework. The correct question is not "What should I do?" but **"What kind of 'Me' is capable of doing this?"**

547 days have passed since October 13, 2023, when I decided to study for the CSAT. A lot of time has passed since my trip to Boston. The exam paper shown above is from that October, where I solved a Grade 11 September Mock Exam without any background knowledge (though I had basic concepts of Math I and II). You can see I failed every question that required even a hint of logical thinking. I scored a Grade 4 by Grade 11 standards. I didn't even touch the last four problems.

If the current me (with Grade 1 scores) could advise the past me (with Grade 4 scores), what would I say? What words would be helpful? What is that "one phrase" needed for a grade hike? **There is no such thing.** You just endure. What would have happened if the past me had spent all his time agonizing over "Why can't I solve this #30?" instead of simply increasing the volume of practice? Can one even define the "reason for failing a specific problem" unless it’s a near-miss or a simple mistake? You can't. If you miss a problem, you just learn it. You look at it again and again, and again, until a problem that once felt monumental feels comfortable.

I believe that is the concept of **"Understanding"** in the CSAT. You must not despair over what you cannot understand. Understanding is not an exclamation mark that pops up instantly during a Night Study session; it is more like a deep, lingering ellipsis that allows you to view the entire structure only after months or years have passed. That is what I believe study and growth are.

The issues with my character will be resolved in a similar way. Sophisticated social behaviors toward others, which feel very awkward at first, will gradually become familiar and become my own. Then, dealing with others will be much easier than it is now. It's the same with time and emotion management. You just try doing what you haven't done before. For example, like my roommate, I could try solving Korean problems as soon as I get out of bed at 6:30 AM without even washing my face, or strictly stop thinking and go to sleep exactly at midnight. I’ll try it once, however hard it is. Once you’ve done it once, you can do it twice. And surely a third time. In that way, you adapt to a new lifestyle and become a more suitable person. It’s the same as discarding my stubbornness and following Kang Ki-won’s methods—like defining Max/Min functions as the sum of positive values within an interval or viewing inverse functions from a new perspective. In fact, there are many sequence problems I can now solve because of that. I might be able to fill my knowledge up to the threshold of a Grade 1 with my own stubbornness. After all, I am a high-tier Korean examinee with burning motivation and high capacity. But I believe the factor that turns "High-tier" into "Top-tier," and "Top-tier" into "Ultra-top-tier," is exactly this kind of contemplation and effort. You follow first, make it yours, adapt to what you weren't doing, and only after becoming a new person do you add your own color. You return to your initial resolve and fill in the scales one by one. Not rushing, but steadily spending the remaining 200 days just as you have, whether others are in the study hall or not. I believe this is the humility and caution required for top-tier entrance exams. A person who is both smart and wise cannot be stopped. I can do this.

### 2. The Sword

There is no need to be dejected just because studying isn't going well. Not everything in the world can be understood rationally. Therefore, one is required to constantly motivate themselves through a proper harmony of reason and emotion.

[Image Placeholder: Posters from *Berserk* and *Demon Slayer*]
*(It is no exaggeration to say that this movie I saw in 8th grade put me into HAFS.)*

I carry a model of the "Dragon Slayer" in my pencil case like a writing tool. For a swordsman, the act of drawing the sword from its sheath (Bal-geom) signifies that the battle has begun. Once the battle starts, someone must die. It’s either you or me. For a student, a pencil case is like a sheath. Opening a book and taking out a mechanical pencil signifies that study has begun. Once study starts, you are bound to learn something new. Because of this symbolic meaning, I carry this heavy sword model and keep it by my side while studying. The price for me missing a CSAT problem is merely a slash of a pen across the paper, but if I were a person who lived by the sword, that slash would have been across my neck. In this way, the metaphor of the sword helps me immerse myself further in my studies and allows me to replace various limits felt in studying with new possibilities.

[Image Placeholder: *Undertale* and *Demon Slayer* soundtracks on a screen]

What practically helps when things are hard are the soundtracks from *Demon Slayer* or *Undertale*. It might be cringeworthy, but I try to mimic the spirit or grit of Rengoku internally without showing it on the outside. In fact, he is 19 in the story, and I am 19 now. Whenever a seriously desperate or sad situation approaches, I purposely fill my head with thoughts in the exaggerated tone that Rengoku might use. Similarly, whenever a moment of defeat—essentially a "Game Over"—is staged in my life, I tell myself: "I can't end it here! Kim Yeon-woo, stay determined..." *Undertale* is a game I played in elementary school, and *Demon Slayer* is an anime I watched in 8th grade. I find comfort in repeating thoughts that an elementary or middle schooler might have whenever I, nearly an adult, find things hard. I can't help it. It’s not something that can be handled rationally. If it constantly motivates me, I have no choice but to use it.

[Image Placeholder: My iPad and phone wallpapers (SNU Veterinary Medicine)]

Recently, I changed my iPad and smartphone wallpapers to the Seoul National University College of Veterinary Medicine logo. I don't think it's a bad thing at all to show my ambition so blatantly and nakedly. It’s because it makes me strive harder to meet that standard. On the right is a study of a mouse brain map by IARPA, which I am interested in. In the future, I want to do work like that—creating computers that think like humans by mimicking brain circuits. The whole world has been obsessed with this for 10 years. Even if it’s a "New World," I’ll be lucky to even be a spectator. Before that, I must move forward humbly and carefully as a "worker." I must find a breakthrough in any situation and focus on what needs to be done.
