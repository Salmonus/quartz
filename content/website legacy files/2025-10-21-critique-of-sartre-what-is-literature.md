# What is Literature? – A Critique of Sartre

**By:** Kim Yeon-woo

**Platform:** Brunch

**Note:** *Unfinished Draft / Independent Essay*

---

I am lying in a graveyard. When you roll around in a graveyard, you get covered in ashes. They are the ashes of the dead. The living have the wisdom of the living, and ghosts have the wisdom of ghosts, but when the living play with ghosts, there is always a tragic reason behind it. It is the role of the critic to reveal that tragedy. Thus, Kim Yeon-woo cannot critique Kim Yeon-woo; Kim Yeon-woo can only critique Sartre, or the other figures buried in that same graveyard.

Therefore, I critique Sartre. However, this critique is not intended to draw out some blind naturalistic value and declare, "This is what French Existentialist literature is." Professors are better at that than I am. In fact, if I were to do that, it would be a form of literary suicide. If I am viewed as a writer, I am a novice, and such grand declarations are exactly what strangle a novice.

Nor do I intend to find the reason why Sartre wrote this text—that is, "a text discussing texts"—in his personal history. Of course, he was quite ugly. He was likely unpopular with women, and perhaps he needed to utilize the psychological knowledge he gained in university to have his way with young women in Parisian salons. It is precisely this kind of narrative that is meaningless. I politely subtitled this "A Critique of Sartre," but that was merely to uphold my pride; in reality, this text is nothing more than "An Acceptance of Sartre." I do not enjoy the act mentioned above—reducing meta-language to a mere book report or reducing style to personal history.

Thus, I am not a "critic." Yet, paradoxically, that makes me the kind of critic Sartre would have found fascinating were he still alive. I know I am in no position to critique Sartre. This is not because I am not a "master of modern Korean literature," nor because I have yet to reject a prestigious award—like the Nobel Prize in Literature that Sartre himself famously refused.

To emphasize again: I am lying in a graveyard. A library is a type of graveyard, and books like *What is Literature?* are tombstones within that graveyard that must be dusted off three times before they can even be read. Look at the library at HAFS; observe the books brought in during its days as a foreign language high school (before 2011) sitting on the shelves, covered in dust, never once opened. The reason is that most of the "ash" is useless. Ash is the residue of burnt flesh. Flesh—the body—is the fuel of language. Language that is not verse is all too easily forgotten. That is the inevitable destination of prose, which utilizes language as a tool. The objects to which the signs once pointed have already vanished.

However, among the many types of prose, one specific kind rises like a phoenix from its own ashes. That is "language about language"—**Meta-language**. Such prose, such sets of signs, use language instrumentally, but because the object they intend to point to is *themselves*, they return to their place even after hitting the target of their era. Like a boomerang.

This is why I have no choice but to accept Sartre. Somehow, I have retreated from the world of the living to the world of the dead, from the plaza to the graveyard—but that graveyard was filled with ashes that had grown cold. Only by digging through those ashes to reach the one warm spot could Kim Yeon-woo find rest. How could I critique it? Sartre is that very resting place! Perhaps one day I will find the courage to take an ember and walk out into the plaza. But not now. If I were ready, I wouldn't be writing this.

Society asks the person who wants to write: "What is your reason for writing?" And the look in the questioner's eye is usually cold. The premise of the question is the cynicism: "If you could have achieved your goal through any other means, why choose writing?" I know. If I were a poet—one who hones intuition, which is the quality of silence—the answer might be easier. Poetry is not a sign pointing to something; it exists as it is, possessing the nature of objects, nature, music, and painting. Prose, however, is bound to point to something, and revealing that "something" carries both personal and social meaning. Intuition is satisfied with scribbling a few words. But prose is a sequence of sentences, and constructing those sentences requires the resolve to make intuition "make sense." That resolve may contain a few elements that are not entirely truthful. Perhaps that is why people came to think of prose writers as liars.

I do not know. *What is Literature?* appears to be a question on the surface, but it is actually a declaration. The title would still make sense if it were "I Shall Now Teach You About Literature." *What could literature be?*—that is a true question. From the very first sentence of this prose, I admitted my ignorance. My reason for writing this is to draft a personal essay for my university applications. Is an essay not a form of literature? Therefore, I find it necessary to step away from the warm spot in the graveyard I have been enjoying and objectify that warmth. Stepping away from Sartre for a moment, what really *is* literature? Here is my thought.

First, a confession. I have only read 10% of *What is Literature?*. This is because the free version of the e-book only provided that much. I originally intended to write after finishing the whole book. However, while riding the subway, reading only 66 pages out of 660 gave me far too much to say. Therefore, if what I say is already covered in the remaining 90%, I apologize; it stems solely from my ignorance.

In my view, there are only "writings about my experience" and "writings not about my experience." I have no interest in the latter. Those are the kinds of writings that turn to ash and disappear when the author dies, and they are not the kind of writing required for admission to U.S. universities. Thus, this text deals only with one half of the Library of Babel: "writings about my experience." There are four types:

1. I do *not* know (my experience).
2. I *know* (my experience).
3. I know (my experience). *You can know it, too.*
4. *You* know (my experience).

The first is **Verse (Poetry)**; the second is **Prose Literature**; the third is the **Rational Essay**; and the fourth is the **Thesis/Paper**. As you move from top to bottom, subjectivity is excluded and objectivity increases. Of course, that subjectivity and objectivity are specifically about "my experience."

It is always refreshing to provide a concrete example after presenting abstract concepts. So, let’s imagine tossing a ball...
