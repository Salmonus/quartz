An individual who is physically strong can inflict damage on an enemy by cutting them directly with a sword, but one can also use strategy and tactics—such as fire or water attacks. One can launch a surprise raid in the middle of the night or send an assassin to kill only the enemy general. In war, it only matters that you win, regardless of the method.

The industrial and research fields are no different from a battlefield. No matter who is gathered, producing a result is what matters. In an AI lab, that means showing performance that exceeds the **State-of-the-Art (SOTA)**; for a startup, it’s about quickly securing seed funding through any round. The "field" is a battlefield where you persuade people and add substance to ideas using agility, wit, and humor, regardless of the process. In that battlefield, a designer’s capacity can sometimes be far more important than a scientist's. Even if the technology doesn't exist yet, if you bluff that it does and persuade people with an elegant prototype and a convincing message, they will gather. You only need to secure enough early-stage technology to support the message. No—you *must* do it that way.

[Image Placeholder: 2023 AI Frontier Competition Awards]

In the **2023 AI Frontier Competition**, we won the Grand Prize. How did a "Jasago" (Autonomous Private High School) student win against gifted and science high school students who are overwhelmingly superior in technical implementation?

1. **First, it must be beautiful.**
2. **"Who I am" is the most important factor.**
3. **However, technology is also important.**

Ultimately, it just has to be beautiful. I’m not talking about the faces of the team members; I mean obsessively pursuing beauty in every detail of reports, PPTs, text, and slides. A 10th-grader created the logo, design concept, system architecture, fonts, and layout of that report above. Imagine an interview. Even if they stutter a bit, if a participant has the looks of a celebrity, the eyes of the judges will stay on them.

The cost-effectiveness and system specifications of LG monitors are praised throughout the industry. On the other hand, Apple’s Mac Pro Display received harsh reviews because it failed to reach professional-grade specs despite its price of millions of won. Apple's "expandability" is notorious for being "trash." Regardless, content creators always have a Mac on their desks, not an LG. Why? Because it’s beautiful. People are ultimately persuaded by emotion rather than reason.

How can one make a beautiful product? Where should one start to make someone gasp in awe—even if they don't fully understand what they are looking at—simply because it is so beautiful?

It starts with **"Me"** (or **"Us"**).

The team name was **"Five High Schoolers."** It was my choice to name it this way. Since high school students were entering the competition, we had to find an idea suitable for high schoolers. It was important to find something that only we knew better than the adult experts in the industry. It had to be an AI that helps with "Performance Assessments" (S 수행평가), and from those target customers and core values, the final design was selected. In my criteria, that’s 70% of the work done. If you have a good message, storytelling, and beautiful design, technology is the next problem.

However, if we had only done that much, we would have struggled to win. It is fundamentally an AI competition, and the algorithm is a major evaluation metric. Initially, the elegant cover and provocative title likely caught the judges' eyes, and the two pages of technical implementation plans I attached finally persuaded them to put us in the finals. In the end, you must be good at both **Reason and Emotion (I-seong & Gam-seong)**.

### The Suneung Struggle

I’ve started to find it difficult to focus on studying for the **CSAT (Suneung)**. Let’s think of it as a war. I have already experienced "real war" (projects/competitions) and explored tens of thousands of ways to win, yet suddenly I am told to perform only repetitive sword-drawing drills and push-ups. I know—I must be good at those too. But all this effort *just* for this?

This is a thought I can only afford because my situation is better than last year. At a Grade 1 level, I can realistically approach every problem, and my mathematical-logical thinking and scientific analysis skills have been sufficiently developed. I am full of confidence that I can interpret any data given the necessary concepts and time. The factors preventing me from a perfect 100 are calculation errors during timed practice and the time shortage caused by "brute-forcing" equations when I don't immediately see the structure of a specific problem. Science (Gwatam) is especially like this. Ultimately, there is also the issue of my daily life patterns.

Let’s think again about why I came here (to the Suneung track). South Korean medical schools are undergraduate programs; therefore, I can complete them faster than in the U.S. (where they are graduate programs) and continue the AI research I truly want to do. I think the me of that time was too naive, not knowing the "end" of the Suneung. There were many times I was angry because the values I consider important clashed with the evaluation criteria. I never look down on others without reason. However, I also know how "foolish" children who have only run toward domestic entrance exam success look in projects without a fixed format. Even when saying the same thing, there is a way to deliver it much more attractively. It’s about digging into the most fundamental "Why?" more creatively and concisely. It is painful to realize I must spend six more years with people who haven't learned such things and have no interest in them. Also, I’ve realized the "stubbornness" I must discard to reach a 100th percentile rather than just a Grade 1. That stubbornness would be a core competency that no one could replace in a real-world setting. Paradoxically, it is of no use for the Suneung.

I’ve decided to take it easy. After all, an average percentile of **96% (Grade 1)** is enough to enter the STEM departments at Seoul National University. I’m not belittling it or looking down on it. I’m not saying it's a given either. However, if I am strict with my basic daily patterns and condition management, and if linear growth of skill occurs until November, it is a score range I can aim for stably. Medical school, of course, requires a score near perfection. Between a 96th percentile and a 100th percentile, there exists a fundamental difference in disposition, far greater than that between a 70th and 96th. A person who believes they must be good at both "Reason and Emotion" is inevitably forced to split their brain. The moment you split your brain, 100% becomes an impossible percentile. Even if I think, *"Just close your eyes for 6 months and immerse yourself in the Suneung,"* the difference in the education I’ve received over the past 12 years is too great.

### Global vs. Domestic Mindset

[Image Placeholder: Elon Musk Quote]

In Korea, if you post the question, "What should I do to join Samsung Electronics?" on a community board, it's hard to expect the same kind of answers as these:

> "I don't care about your degree." — **Elon Musk** > "Focus on what you need to learn and start from there."
> "Become an exceptionally influential person in something very related to technology. A degree might help?"

You can see that the starting point of the thought process is different. Of course, if you actually go to Neuralink’s LinkedIn and scan the employees' academic backgrounds, they are staggering. But that is the result of starting from an individual’s own questions and interests—it’s not about stacking "specs" and cutting them like a blade, as is done in Korea. This difference in mindset makes me hesitate between a Grade 1 and a perfect 100. People who just *do* it without questioning are at such an advantage; they have overtaken me.

There is no single answer to this question. However, Jobs should design and attract investment like Jobs, and Wozniak should code like Wozniak. There is no time to hesitate at that boundary. If you can do both, do both. The mindset of "I'm good at this, so I'm bad at that" is not desirable. In any case, it is right to do what must be done.

In competitions between teenagers, there is a sense of being forgiven even if you are technically lacking. To achieve the same success even without that leeway, I must not go easy on myself now and should proudly become a member of the academic community. I can do it.
