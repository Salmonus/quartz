### Introduction: From *The Magic Mountain* (Vol. 1, pp. 53-54)

Hans Castorp was no genius, nor was he a fool. If we refrain from calling him an "ordinary man," it is not because of his intellect or character, but out of a sense of awe toward his destiny. We feel it is right to grant his fate a superhuman significance. His intelligence was sufficient to complete a vocational high school course without much effort. Yet, for him, the idea of "working hard"—regardless of the situation—was unthinkable. This was not because he feared the suffering of useless effort, but because he felt no absolute necessity for it. Perhaps it is because he vaguely sensed this lack of necessity that we hesitate to classify him as ordinary.

A human being lives not only as an individual but also, consciously or unconsciously, as a member of their era. Even if one sees the universal foundations of their life as absolute facts—as Hans Castorp did—one might vaguely foresee that their mental stability is an illusion if those foundations were to crumble even slightly. We all imagine personal goals, hopes, and prospects, and we exert endless effort to achieve them. However, if the impersonal era surrounding us—despite its outward vitality—is internally devoid of hope or prospect, and if that era remains silent to our conscious or unconscious questions regarding the "ultimate" or "superhuman" meaning of all effort, such a situation will induce a form of paralysis in any sincere human being.

This paralysis may spread from the mental and moral aspects to the physical and logical. If a person seeks to achieve remarkable results beyond their given capacity when the era provides no satisfactory answer to the question "Why?", that person must possess a rare, heroic spiritual solitude and a persistent vitality. Hans Castorp possessed none of these. Therefore, no matter how politely we phrase it, he was destined to remain an "ordinary man."

This long observation serves as a reference for understanding not only the internal state of this young man during his student years but also his subsequent choices in profession.

### I. Points of Caution

#### I-1. Two Short Stories

**I-1-a. *An Indian Biography*, Hermann Hesse**
In this story, Dasa, a prince raised as a cowherd to escape a murderous stepmother, eventually regains his throne, weds the beautiful Pravati, and has a son, Ravana. After losing everything—his son to war and his dignity to a prison cell—he wakes up. He realizes he is actually standing by a spring with a water jar in his hands. He had only been away for a few minutes to fetch water for a Yoga practitioner.

The years of royalty, war, and grief were all **Maya (Illusion)**. Dasa is overwhelmed by the void. He realizes that even the "real" jars and moss around him are made of the same dream-stuff. Exhausted by the "devilish" cycle of experience that makes a fool of man, Dasa realizes that death is merely a short breath before the dance begins again. He chooses to fill the jar and return to the service of his master, for obedience and service are healthier than the burden of ruling.

**I-1-b. *Legend of an Indian King*, Hermann Hesse**
A young king seeks the ultimate truth. He masters scriptures and performs extreme penance, fasting for 40 days until his eyes put the Brahmins to shame. He invites the sages to debate, but the more they talk about *Brahma* and *Atman*, the more he feels like a child playing with toys while the truth remains untouched.

Eventually, the King sinks into himself, closing his senses. He finds the truth as a pure light within, becoming one with the creator and the creation. He leaves his clothes, his city, and his kingdom, entering the forest naked to disappear forever.

#### I-2. There Are No Adventures: The Affectation of Antoine Roquentin

There is a saying, "writing in one stroke" (*Il-pil-hwi-ji*). People often value a text written all at once more than one that has been heavily edited. I believe this is nothing more than awe for raw talent. Editing is naturally more painful. The human spirit has RAM (emotions) and an HDD (memory). Emotions are volatile; the words of yesterday often lack the resonance they had when first conceived. Thus, continuing a single piece over several days is a task for a meticulous "Iron Man"—one who can plan even their own emotions.

I use writing as a **"Save Button."** Even if I were to lose my life tomorrow, this writing allows someone else to inherit the essence of my task (the progress of the game called Life).

In Jean-Paul Sartre’s *Nausea*, Antoine Roquentin realizes that one must either **live** or **tell stories**.

> "When we live, nothing happens. The settings change, people come and go. There is no beginning. Days are added to days without reason... But when we tell of a life, everything changes. We tell it from the end. The end is there, invisible but present, giving the beginning its value."

Roquentin is an outsider, a man without a job or a wife, floating on the margins of society because he recognizes the limits of life. Writing is not a "stroke of luck" for him; it is a tragic necessity to face the "Nausea" of existence. Sartre himself, like Wittgenstein and Russell, faced the paradox of the philosopher: the temptation to think one knows a "better path" while in reality, one is just piecing together an affectation. True sages don't put on airs; they preach the limits of the system and return to raw, unadorned life.

### II. The Adult (成人) and The Sage (聖人)

#### II-1. Modernity as a Deal (Excerpt from Harari’s *Homo Deus*)

"Modernity is a deal... The entire contract can be summarised in a single phrase: **humans agree to give up meaning in exchange for power.** Until modern times, most cultures believed humans played a part in some great cosmic plan... Life had meaning but restricted power. Modern culture rejects this. Life has no script, no playwright, and no meaning... The modern world does not believe in purpose, only in cause."

#### II-2. The "Salauds" (Bastards)

Sartre critiques the "Salauds"—the Bourgeoisie who feel they have a "right to everything" and feel no existential anxiety. They rely on "perfect moments" or historical research (like Roquentin’s study of the Marquis de Rollebon) to escape the "now." These are inauthentic ways to overcome Nausea. They live the lives of the dead, clinging to portraits and statues (like that of Impétraz), unable to face their own naked existence.

#### II-3. *Une Vie* (A Life)

Maupassant’s *Une Vie* (often translated in Japan and Korea as *A Woman's Life*) depicts the watercolor landscape of a rural noblewoman’s life, contrasting with the cubist abstraction of *Nausea*.

I will soon be 20. Legally, I will be an **Adult (成人)**. But this is far from being a **Sage (聖人)**. Whether it is Jeanne (blinded by love), the Self-Taught Man (blinded by books), or Ahab (blinded by revenge), all are legal adults, yet all are blind. Roquentin knows he is blind, yet he calls it "Nausea."

Wittgenstein said: *"The meaning of the world must lie outside the world. In the world, everything is as it is, and everything happens as it does happen: in it no value exists."* I have been given responsibilities: the academic responsibility of the CSAT and the biological responsibility of Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT). I have known for a long time that modernity is a contract—not one signed with a seal upon legal adulthood, but one signed through **action**.

When I visited the National Museum of Korea, I preferred viewing the exhibits in reverse—from the Joseon Dynasty back to the Paleolithic. I wanted to see the process of humanity regaining **versatility** over narrow **utility**. A hand-ax is simple and beautiful; it can cut, pierce, or pound. The moment we specialized it into a spear, we sacrificed versatility for utility.

Steve Jobs, Zen Buddhism, and the Hippie culture all share a standard of perfection: **perfection is not when there is nothing left to add, but when there is nothing left to take away.** While we cannot tell the modern factory worker to pick up a stone tool, we must not lose the **"Spirit of the Hand-Ax."** If we use tools with the spirit of the *Whole Earth Catalog* and ask "Why?" for every element of civilization we adopt, we might fill the void of meaning Harari described.

### III. The Ultimate Business Model

#### III-1. Market Fit (PMF) and the BM Canvas

At the KAIST Gifted Center for Entrepreneurship, I learned the **Business Model Canvas**: defining customers, value propositions, channels, and revenue structures. I had a talent for this because it aligned with my core belief: that essence is more important than form. However, a week-long camp provided no opportunity to scale that model. Then, I entered HAFS.

#### III-2. CSAT, Corporations, and Korea: Iterative Expansion

What is the CSAT? It is a structure that assigns talent to the capital they most prefer. Korea is a Republic of Large Corporations (Chaebols). A large corporation is an organization where the business model is fixed and has undergone decades of iterative expansion. They rarely do anything new unless they are acquiring a startup.

The most important quality of a founder is not just finding "Product-Market Fit," but **Scalability**—the ability to repeat and expand a business model. The CSAT is no different. Once you go through the curriculum, you understand the "essence" of the Evaluation Institute (KICE). You reach a level of Grade 1. But the "Elite" exams (Medical Track) divide the 96th to 100th percentile even more finely.

Why? Because a corporate business model is not made to run once. From Galaxy 3 to 20, from iPhone 1 to 16, they repeat the process of refining a proven product until it is mind-numbing. Korea's CSAT is designed to train people for this chaebol republic. **Even if you know the essence, you must do it again.** Patience and grit—these are the competencies of an entrepreneur, a doctor, and an examinee. If you cannot do it again, you haven't truly mastered it. If you have conviction, you can repeat the cycle for the entire scope of the exam. That is entrepreneurship, that is scholarship, and that is life. Mastery of the essence can only be proven by repeating it—stubbornly and honestly, like a hand-ax.
