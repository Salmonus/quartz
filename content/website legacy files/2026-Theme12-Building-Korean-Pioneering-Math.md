### I. Science (Gwatam) is Always Short on Time

The more time is lacking, the more perfectly one must master the material. This is because there is no time to "come back and poke at it" later.

For instance, in the recent March Mock, I couldn't solve Math #22 or #28 immediately; I had to come back to them. My method was either eliminating a negative number that couldn't be the answer for a subjective question among two possibilities or finding an odd-function shape that could produce a  from the choices, even if it wasn't strictly related to the conditions. I got the answers right, but if Math were as time-pressed as Science, these would have remained as wrong answers. I didn't approach them properly at first sight; I merely exploited the constraints of the paper to pick the "scoring" answer. This is only possible when time is abundant, as it is in Math. Moving forward, I should not do this even in Math.

Mastery is more important than whether one "can" solve it, and the ability to operate within the 30-minute limit is essential. Without that operational skill, no matter how many problems you solve by unit, you cannot get a high score. Furthermore, it is crucial to review thoroughly so the material becomes entirely your own. While this applies to every subject, Science is something you must internalize to the point that it "pops out" instantly—otherwise, it’s no better than not solving it at all, because you'll end up with the effect of "vaguely avoiding the correct answer."

Recognizing these facts, I have concluded that I must pour the time of day when I am most immersed into my two Science electives. That time is "Night Study" (Yaja). Therefore, I have decided to invest the entirety of Night Study 1 and 2 into Physics I and Chemistry I until the May Mock. If these extremely low scores repeat in the next Provincial Office exam, there is no reason to stick with Science until the June KICE Mock. It would be wiser to switch to Social Studies (the so-called "Satham-run"). I will strive to ensure that doesn't happen.

### II. Approaching Korean Past Papers Consistently (Actually, it’s not "Studying Korean")

I thought I did poorly, but it turns out the cut-offs for Korean and Math were lower than expected. My Math score is at Grade 1, and my Korean was only 2 points away from a Grade 1. Reaching the threshold of a Grade 1 without finishing a single lecture series or a single past paper workbook means the starting point is excellent.

[Image Placeholder: Kim Seung-ri's 'All-O-Ka' Lecture Capture]

It's not that I can't read. It's that I am not yet familiar with the recurring flow of KICE (Evaluation Institute) texts and not prepared for the "Viewpoints" (Bo-gi) and question prompts through past paper analysis. Reading comprehension itself is hard to raise within 200 days, but an instructor can teach you *what kind of texts* KICE selects. Training every morning will improve my "exam-taking ability" and condition management, as I mentioned in my previous review.

I have loved reading and writing since elementary school. In terms of lifetime volume, the time I spent reading books would overwhelmingly surpass the time spent solving math problems. However, KICE exams are different. I received a Grade 1 in Math, which I trained for, and a Grade 2 in Korean, which I did not. In Science, which I trained for most intensely, I got a Grade 4; yet in Korean, the score doesn't drop significantly even without study, but it also doesn't show quick improvement even with effort. Knowing this, I judged that starting now, at the senior level, is appropriate.

**Actually, it’s not "studying Korean."**

Among the parts I haven't studied yet, I am weak in Classical Poetry and Classical Prose. In classical literature, there is a concept of "motifs," making it convenient to analyze works through that structure. Interestingly, a year ago, when I read the original Latin text of the *Aeneid*, knowing the tradition of epic poetry—appealing to the Muse for creation—was essential to interpreting the line *"Musa, mihi causas memora"* (Muse, recount to me the reasons). I had to know why Aeolus was angry and why Juno sank the ships to write proper answers on the AP Latin exam.

Classical literature is always like this. You must know the related myths and background before reading the work; that *is* the capacity to decode classical literature. Therefore, I judge that the CSAT's method of teaching classical literature is appropriate. When I try to read something in Latin again later, I will have to re-examine Greek and Roman mythology. If I challenge the *Upanishads*, I will need to know Indian and Hindu culture. It’s better to read my own country's classics before reading those of others.

How should modern literature be interpreted? In fact, it *cannot* be "interpreted." Opinions differ from reader to reader, so how can a fixed interpretation be offered? One must simply filter out the things that can be clearly distinguished according to KICE's focus. I don't "like" that, but regardless of preference, it is a basic reading skill and these are questions I must get right.

What about Non-fiction (Reading)? In fact, it *cannot* be "studied." An individual's reading comprehension is a constant value, and there are as many ways to read a text as there are people. Still, I think Kim Seung-ri is right. There are flows—like "Opposition structures" or "Chronological structures"—that KICE texts rely on. If you know these structural methods, you can think proactively while reading: *"They’ll ask about A vs. B, so I should categorize everything as A or B,"* or *"They’ll ask about past-present-future, so I should check what changes occurred at each point."* If I throw a "pre-emptive strike" at Korean passages with this consistent framework—knowing what KICE will ask before reading—it will surely raise my May Mock score, even if it feels far from the act of actually revising one's knowledge and expanding one's worldview through reading. Moreover, it's not entirely detached from real reading; predicting what will come next is a basic action everyone performs. While the "CSAT evil" is that this attitude is overemphasized to the point of being mandatory, one who cannot overlook such a minor flaw is not in the right mindset to climb the ladder.

Therefore, CSAT Korean cannot really be "interpreted" or "studied." It’s not something you interpret or study in the first place. You recognize it and prepare for it. If you call that study, then it is study, but an individual's absolute Korean capacity is likely to remain fixed even after the CSAT. Even if it increases, it's not because you studied "CSAT Korean," but simply because you read texts. If you had encountered the same text in a newspaper or a library, it would have raised your Korean capacity to the same degree. With that mindset, let’s approach Korean past papers consistently.

> **Per varios casus, per tot discrimina rerum tendimus in Latium.**
> *(Through various accidents, through so many perils of fortune, we make our way to Latium.)*

"Per" is a preposition meaning "through." It doesn't mean "I head to Latium *despite* these problems," but "I head to Latium *through* these problems." To put it another way, without the *varios casus* and *tot discrimina*, I couldn't reach *Latium*.

The part I felt was lacking while burning with passion for AI from 7th to 10th grade was exactly what CSAT Math solved. In that sense, I have gained more capacity to create AI through CSAT Math. Regardless of math percentiles or universities, one can only "do" AI when they possess unshakeable confidence in the mathematical-logical thinking they have deployed. There is no CSAT math problem with 18 conditions, but one must manage an AI service with 18 directories with consistent thinking power.

In conclusion, the capacity to thoroughly examine a logical decision I've made and say it is "definitely correct" is a vital skill, regardless of my position or contribution. In that sense, I should not get things like this #30 wrong. If I don't even know that there are three more cases besides , there is no way I could propose a new framework for AI. The thoughts I have are the Truth, and what I judge to be mathematically correct is the "future-oriented theme" that will appear on the CSAT. I am good at math, and I do not doubt my objective, logical judgment... Reaching this level is necessary to stand in a position to create something creatively, rather than just being in the "Survival" stage.
