# Senior Story (2): The Complementary Relationship Between Problem Posing and Problem Solving

**By:** Kim Yeon-woo

**Platform:** Brunch

**TL;DR:** The ability to pose a problem and the ability to solve a problem are complementary to each other.

---

### I. A Journey from Questioning to Discourse (Problem Posing)

It seems that truly important questions often make a person look like a fool. It would be ideal if the magnitude of one's questions were proportional to how "smart" one appears or directly linked to economic rewards, but in most cases, the opposite is true.

For example, consider the question: *"How do we distinguish the arrow symbol in a function from the arrow symbol in a logical proposition?"* I remember being humiliated in front of my class for asking this at a large private academy in the 7th grade. However, that was actually an observation that pierced the heart of formal logic—the fact that a formal system and its interpretation are not necessarily identical. It was a remarkably extraordinary doubt for a 13-year-old child just beginning "Math (Lower)."

But because it was a question that **posed a problem** rather than **solved one**, it was perceived in Korean society as a challenge to authority rather than a sign of brilliance. Public opinion—evaluating the speaker's "frame" rather than the content of the statement with remarks like *"Are you good at math?"* or *"Stop acting like you know everything"*—instantly extinguishes a child's pure curiosity and tells them to *"wait until college to think about such things."* In this environment, a perfect score without original questions is seen as superior to a 92 with them; that 8-point deficit is blamed on the "distractions" of the one who poses questions. Thus, the child grows into a high schooler and eventually an adult. Even when they encounter someone like **Andrew Wiles**, who decided at age 10 to prove Fermat’s Last Theorem and finished it at 41, they consume him only as a "genius from another world," failing to recognize that his journey started with a different kind of question.

While the IQ of high achievers might be slightly higher than average, and their families might be wealthier or offer more opportunities, I believe these things only assist the journey—they are not its core. What truly matters is whether the problems posed in childhood are internalized as **core beliefs** during ego formation. Questions like *"Why are humans unhappy?"*, *"How can we teach a machine to speak?"*, or *"What is the difference between men and women?"* have no answer keys. Society does not approve them, evaluate them positively, or provide instant feedback. You cannot share them with friends while laughing or post them on Instagram.

The Github projects explored in a dark study hall, the solitary lunches spent making time to study Latin, and the walks taken alone to structure my thoughts—these become memories that help me acquire my own sophisticated language. The word **"Dok-bo-jeok" (獨步的 - Unrivaled)** has no inherent meaning of "excellence." It simply refers to the **"appearance (的) of walking (步) alone (獨)."** We eventually have no choice but to call those who endure loneliness by using their internalized doubts as a shield "unrivaled."

The language of those who walked alone eventually becomes discourse, philosophy, and standards. This was true for **Turing, Gödel, Hinton, and Hofstadter**. Questions like *"Can machines think?"* or *"Is mathematics complete?"* have the power to gather people, form discourse, and ultimately open a new era. Genius is not just a spark within a system of solving problems; it is accompanied by the **psychological stamina** to hold on tight to questions that are too far ahead of their time. Even as a CSAT (Suneung) student, I have fought to protect "my questions." I have strived to cultivate the ability to pose problems—the ability to redefine the question itself rather than just following the previous generation or choosing the most "appropriate" answer.

### II. A Transaction Defined by Right Answers and College Admissions (Problem Solving)

I am not trying to make the mistake of blindly denying the existing entrance exam ecosystem or deifying a baseless "spirit of inquiry." Rather, I recognize the limit: all academic communities begin with a question, but if that question is not institutionalized and quantified, it cannot survive as a system. The term "standardized frame" may seem to carry negative connotations, but for some, it is an **irreplaceable formal foothold** that allows them to rapidly absorb elusive concepts.

The CSAT was that for me. I am convinced that the relative evaluation curve, the meticulous categorization of types, and the training to logicize my framework for time management in the Science section have propelled me from a "quirky child" to a "young researcher with a unique worldview." I learned that the correct attitude is not saying, *"I have a special dream, so my scores don't matter,"* but rather, *"How can I use both hands freely despite the constraints?"* When I applied to switch from the International Track to the Natural Science Track at HAFS and began solving the same past papers as everyone else under the same conditions, I finally learned that true uniqueness lies in the attitude of not losing one's will even when there is nothing "special" about the circumstances. I realized that a high-stakes exam like the CSAT is the best version of reductionist education—one that can partially replace the four pillars of US admissions (GPA, EC, Essay, Rec) in an academic sense. I learned that a single correct answer I write myself can be worth more than multiple recommendation letters written by others, and that taking the CSAT is akin to writing the most refined essay to introduce myself. A researcher who cannot absorb even the most refined discourse (the curriculum) has no right to light any new torch.

### III. Core Beliefs Determine Psychological Stamina (Posing + Solving = Existential Foundation)

Nevertheless, a person who possesses both the ability to pose problems and the ability to solve them is bound to be lonely and intensely busy. This is because they are responsible for the questions of the previous generation while simultaneously laying the groundwork for the next. The motivation to sustain this work is usually only one thing: when their question is a **core belief**, not just a peripheral one. It is a state where it doesn't feel like "it would be nice to solve this while making a living," but rather, "I don't feel alive unless I do this."

To such people, telling them to *"worry about those things in college"* is like telling them to *"hold your breath for an hour."* Questions related to ego formation are not the kind that can be postponed. The moment they are delayed, one feels their existential foundation shaking and faces a strange dissociation of body and mind.

The reason I spend my limited time as an examinee to share these drafts is the same. If I don't do this, my brain refuses to allocate energy even when I open a mock exam in the study hall. Conversely, the moment I complete my own narrative, structure my pain, and universalize my message in a shareable form like this, I gain the power to move forward without needing coffee. Though not an official term, I want to call this **"Psychological Stamina."** The pleasure of becoming a Being—neither a Functional Subject nor a Symbolic Object—is a joy entirely different from external rewards. This is a realm that can only be reached by an inquirer who respects both the posing and the solving of problems. For this, I will continue to press on.
