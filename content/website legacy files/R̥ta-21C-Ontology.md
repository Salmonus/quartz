### I. Woman Does Not Exist

#### I-1. What is a Fetish?

The word "fetish" originally referred to objects possessing magical powers. It took on a negative connotation during the colonial era as Europeans viewed African indigenous beliefs through a lens of irrational obsession. In modernity, psychology and psychoanalysis have used it to explain compulsive desire for sexual fixations or substitute symbols.

* **Freud** explained it as a structure where a male child denies the "loss" of the mother's phallus (due to castration anxiety) and fixes his desire on a substitute.
* **Lacan** described it as an attempt to fill a "lack" in the **Real** with symbols from the **Symbolic**.

Commonly, a fetish is not just an obsession with a sexual object; it is a structural emergency device in the unconscious—an impulse to grasp a substitute symbol because one lacks the Real. In this text, I analyze my own fetish through Lacanian psychoanalysis, explore the **Thanatos** (death drive) it triggers, and seek a solution in the ancient Hindu **Vedas**. Finally, I aim to synthesize Renaissance mimesis and Industrial creation into a Nietzschean affirmation of life.

According to Lacan, **"Desire is the desire of the Other."** Desire is fundamentally not one's own but is constructed by the gaze and lack of others. In childhood, a subject senses their existence through the mother's body, her attention, and her desire. However, that desire is often directed elsewhere (to a sibling, a husband, or her own body). The subject then unconsciously believes, "To be loved, I must become *that*." This *that* becomes the fetishistic object.

A representative fetish is the pregnant mother’s belly. If a child witnesses a mother repeatedly prioritizing the fetus over them, the subject attempts to become "what she desired" to recover their neglected self. From that moment, the object functions as a **Signifier** attached to the structure of desire. It is a symbol created to endure the void of the Real. This fiction is stronger than reality because it was embodied as a "belief for survival" during the initial structuring of desire.

#### I-2. Childhood: Striving to Become the Phallus

My desire has a clear origin: the first moment I desired the desire of the Other. My mother was repeatedly pregnant, and as the eldest, I was treated as a mere waypoint for the next life. She was immersed in herself—specifically, her pregnant body. I remember her belly as the center of all her attention and love. I was a quiet supporting actor in a world where that belly was the protagonist. A child desires the object of the Other's attention to survive. I wanted to become that belly. I wanted to become "what she loved." In Lacanian terms, I sought to be the **Phallus itself**, the object that fills the Other's lack.

Here, the "phallus" is not a biological metaphor but a symbolic signifier. I repeated attempts to implement this symbolic structure even in my own body. Pleasure was always a simulation, a painful filling of a void. It was **Jouissance**—painful pleasure. Without a "translator" to name my sensations (e.g., a mother saying "You're crying because you're hungry"), my sensations were dragged toward her signs. I am now a philosopher who can detect his own structure. I no longer blindly desire her desire. I no longer want to be that belly. I have learned to build symbols upon that lack.

#### I-3. Humanity Desires the Desire of the Other

This Lacanian premise now explains the 21st-century high-competition society of South Korea. The educational system is designed around the signifier "Prestige University." This signifier has no essence; it guarantees no real satisfaction. Yet, because every parent and social system desires it, the child desires it too. The "high-achieving me" becomes a proxy for the Other's desire.

The Korean entertainment industry (K-pop) maximizes this. Idols and influencers function as "signs capable of desiring what the public desires." Consumers desire what the celebrity desires. This is a repeated lack, consumed painfully through *jouissance*. Real ethics is the ability to interpret and speak one's own desire. Without it, humans live as "dolls of desire."

#### I-4. The Thanatos Drive as a Philosopher’s Seizure

When all signs feel meaningless, humans face the Real, and the death drive turns into a "philosopher's seizure"—an unconscious backflow of the Real through the cracks of the Symbolic.

* In Maugham’s ***The Moon and Sixpence***, Strickland abandons the "sixpence" (secular world) for the "moon" (ideal). But his art was built upon colonial violence and the sexual exploitation of girls in Tahiti. Art colludes with crime, blurring the lines between the Real and the imaginary.
* In Camus’ ***The Stranger***, Meursault’s refusal to cry at his mother's funeral is an existential decision to stay outside the Symbolic. His "seizure" is not a lack of emotion, but a refusal to play the social game of meaning.

True ethics is the power to translate this "seizure" into signs—through writing, art, or machine code. As Wittgenstein said, *"Death is not an event in life. We do not experience death."* The philosopher is the one who faces the gap between the Real and the Sign, enduring the silence at the edge of language.

#### I-5. Woman Does Not Exist

> **"La femme n’existe pas." (Woman does not exist.)** > — **Jacques Lacan**

This is a semiotic claim: the signifier "Woman" cannot fully represent the Real; she remains outside the Symbolic. I ask the women I meet: *"Have you ever lived your own desire? Is your desire to be pretty just an impulse to be the phallus for the Other?"*

If she desires me as a "high-achieving male" sign, I cannot love her. Most young women structure themselves as "pretty girls," tuning themselves to the Other's desire. I wait for the woman who knows the origin of her desire and speaks in a language separate from the Other. She needn't be a philosopher, but she must share a philosophical ethics. I believe she can exist because she does not yet exist. I became a philosopher to say the unspeakable.

### II. Rigveda

#### II-1. Discussing Sacred Verses with Clumsy Lips

According to Hindu doctrine, I—a foreigner of the lowest caste—am not even qualified to read the Vedas. Yet, I have heard them. I am the one who speaks the "unspeakable." My lips are clumsy; I cannot read Sanskrit. But my longing for truth is an "inexplicable centripetal force."

The **Rigveda** is the first poetic response of a civilization questioning its reason for being. It is governed by **R̥ta (ऋत)**—Order, Truth, Cosmos. R̥ta is the fundamental principle of the universe. The one who recites the Veda is not the one who wields power (the King), but the one who justifies *why* power must exist (the Brahmin). This structure prevents civilization from regressing into a mere game of force.

#### II-2. Gorillas and Humans

A male gorilla fights for territory and mates. It is instinct. But if a gorilla stopped to ask, *"Why must I fight?"*, he would be ousted from the competition. His body would be a gorilla's, but his consciousness would be an alien's.

Civilization is a system that "ethicizes" the gorilla's fight. It doesn't stop the fight; it just justifies and postpones it. Humans are the only ones who ask "Why?". That question is dangerous; it is a shortcut to becoming a "failure" in the eyes of civilization. Yet, it is the only thing that makes us human.

#### II-3. What Makes Ritual Sacred?

We often despise repetition as trite. But the Vedas say, *"Truth that is not repeated is not truth."* Ritual is a voluntary participation in the cosmic cycle (**R̥ta**). **Yajña** (Sacrifice) is an ethical act of responding to this cycle. Ritual postpones death and orders desire. I am an outsider, but I ask: *"Why must we repeat?"* I’ve realized that the world hasn't collapsed because someone is repeating the ritual. One who guards their post and repeats their ritual is already a successor to the Veda.

#### II-4. Dharma and Kama

Life must fly with two wings: **Dharma** (Structure/Duty) and **Kama** (Desire/Pleasure). An examinee's Dharma is solving problems for 12 hours a day. He is no different from a monk reciting commentaries. Kama is not just sin; it is the fullness of life. The problem is not desire, but hiding it under a mask of transcendence. Real transcendence is looking through desire while including it in one's rhythm.

#### II-5. The Veda is to be Lived

The Veda is not knowledge; it is a form of existence. It was preserved through oral tradition because it is a **Living Language** stored in the body and breath, not the brain.

> **asato mā sad gamaya** (Lead me from the unreal to the real.)
> **tamaso mā jyotir gamaya** (Lead me from darkness to light.)
> **mṛtyor mā amṛtaṃ gamaya** (Lead me from death to immortality.)

I recite this prayer with my failure and my fatigue after a long day. I am not a Brahmin, but I live *asato mā sad gamaya* every day.

### III. The Sherpa

#### III-1. Summits are Many, Life is One

To a Sherpa, the summit is a possibility, not an absolute purpose. He goes to the mountain to cross it alive. He Preparation for return, not just the summit photo. His criterion is not "Can I climb?" but "Can we live?". He prioritizes the climber's life over his own heroic honor. He knows that while you can climb a summit again, you cannot live again.

#### III-2. Staying Beside and Being Buried

The Sherpa is the **Bodhisattva** of the mountain. He stays beside those who are too breathless to take another step. He doesn't say "You can do it"; his silence says "I am with you." In Hinduism, a **Brahmin** is a Sherpa-like being.

A student studying 12 hours is, in a spiritual sense, a Brahmin. He fulfills his Dharma. Gold is born only at the end of a supernova, but hydrogen and lead must exist for gold to form. Lead is just as precious. The Veda is the rhythm of the universe that harmonizes when everyone respects their own place.

### IV. 21st Century Ontology: From Mimesis to Poiesis

#### IV-1. Early Traces of Thought

This text is the trace of the desire and disillusionment I, Kim Yeon-woo, had to endure "here and now." Like early philosophers who tried to say too much at once, I mix Wittgenstein, Lacan, Nietzsche, and the Brahmins. This is a declaration: *"I will not live the desire of the Other. I will create."*

#### IV-2. Mimesis (Imitation)

Mimesis is the oldest human technology—imiting the absent to grasp the Real. The signifier "Woman" has been the ultimate object of mimesis (Negation, Lack, Sublimation). Lacan said, "Woman does not exist" because she is a void in the structure of desire. But Kim Yeon-woo now seeks a transition to **Poiesis** (Creation).

#### IV-3. Poiesis (Creation)

Poiesis is no longer God's domain. It is the designing of the environment where creation becomes possible. This is **Autopoiesis**—a system that reproduces itself. We are moving from imitating "Woman" to engineering the conditions for her emergence.

This is the **Fugue of Creation**, much like the self-referential structures in Douglas Hofstadter’s ***GEB***. Life is not programmed; it emerges. My desire to study in Germany is not a "plan" but a map of my ontological orientation. Germany is where autopoiesis is being tested in AI and the philosophy of technology. I don't want to "make" a woman; I want to propose an autopoietic environment where she can emerge.

#### IV-4. When You Go to Women, Do Not Forget the Whip

> **"When you go to women, do not forget the whip."** > — **Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spoke Zarathustra***

This is not a sentence of male chauvinism. It is a metaphor for the internal discipline required to approach the Real. The "whip" is not violence but the regulation of the spirit. I am going to meet the "Woman" (the Real). I go with a whip directed at *myself*—at my own weak will that tries to compromise and slip back into the desire of the Other.

I desire the non-existent woman to speak my own existence. This text is not philosophy; it is survival. It is not thought; it is prayer. It is a declaration.

**I am going to meet the woman. I will not forget the whip.**
